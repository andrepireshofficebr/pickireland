Buying guide: how to choose

Why do bedrooms fog up faster than any other room in an Irish house?

An adult breathes out roughly half a litre of water vapour overnight, and two people sharing a room double that, all released into a space that's closed off, unheated once the radiator goes down, and often fitted with older single glazing that runs cold to the touch. That combination is exactly what condensation needs: warm moist air meeting a cold surface. It's why bedroom windows are wet by morning even in houses that seem dry everywhere else. A dehumidifier pulls that moisture out of the air before it can land on the glass or the wall behind the headboard, usually the coldest, least ventilated corner in the room and the first place mould shows up on wallpaper or paint. Two adults sharing a double bed in a poorly ventilated room can push local humidity noticeably higher than the rest of the house by morning, even in a home that otherwise reads dry.

How much difference does 32dB versus 40dB actually make at night?

A jump from 32dB to 40dB sounds trivial on a spec sheet but is roughly a doubling in perceived loudness, and it's the difference between a hum you forget about and one that keeps you awake. Compressor units cycle on and off and often have an audible click at each transition; desiccant and Peltier-style compact units tend to run more consistently but can have a higher-pitched fan note that some people find more intrusive than a low hum. Look for a stated dB figure measured at one metre, not just the word 'quiet' in the listing, and treat anything above about 38dB as a machine for daytime use rather than overnight beside a bed. Where possible, listen to the unit before buying — a showroom floor model, or a video review with the sound turned up, gives a far more honest sense of how intrusive the compressor's cycling click actually is than any number printed on a spec sheet.

Is a small 6–8L dehumidifier enough for an Irish bedroom?

A typical Irish bedroom is 10–14 square metres with a ceiling around 2.4m, giving a volume of roughly 25–34 cubic metres — small enough that a compact 6–8L/day unit reaches its target humidity within an hour or two of switching on, faster than a large machine would in the same space. Buying a big-house 20L unit for a single bedroom is wasted capacity and, worse, wasted noise, since a bigger compressor generally runs louder to move more air. Size the machine to the room it will actually live in, not to some future scenario where you might move it downstairs; a second small unit is cheaper than one oversized one and quieter in the room that matters most. The one exception is a bedroom that doubles as an occasional drying space for a load of washing, where the added moisture genuinely justifies stepping up to an 8–10L/day unit rather than staying at the bottom of the compact range.

Tank size versus how often you'll actually empty it

A 1–2 litre tank in an actively damp bedroom can fill within a day, which means a full-shut-off safety feature isn't optional — it's the only thing stopping the unit running dry and doing nothing useful while you're at work. Realistically, tanks under 2L suit a room that's mildly humid rather than genuinely wet; if condensation is a nightly event on the window, budget for emptying daily or look at a model with a continuous-drain hose option even in a bedroom, run discreetly to a bathroom next door. Check how the tank comes out and whether it has a handle and a spout, because an awkward tank you dread emptying is a tank that quietly stops getting emptied. A model with a clear indicator light and an audible alert when full removes most of the guesswork, which matters more in a bedroom than anywhere else in the house, since a silently overflowing tank is easy to miss until it's already happened overnight.

Apartment realities: no hose point, thin walls, rental rules

Rented Irish apartments rarely have a floor drain or an obvious point to run a continuous hose, which pushes most tenants toward tank-only compact units regardless of how wet the room gets — factor that into capacity choice, since you'll be emptying by hand every time. Thin partition walls in older conversions and some newer builds mean a machine's compressor hum can travel through to a neighbouring room, so noise rating matters even more than in a house. Check your tenancy agreement before drilling anything or running a hose through a window seal, and stick to a plug-in unit that needs no fixed installation — it's the only category guaranteed not to cause a deposit dispute when you move out. Where a landlord allows it, a small tray or mat under the unit is worth adding regardless of tank size, purely as insurance against an accidental spill onto a wooden or laminate floor.

Frequently asked questions

Is it safe to leave a dehumidifier running overnight in a bedroom?

Yes, provided the model has auto shut-off when the tank fills or the target humidity is reached, which almost every dehumidifier sold in Ireland today includes as standard. The main practical risk isn't safety so much as noise waking you or a full tank causing the unit to run pointlessly until morning. Keep it clear of bedding and curtains so airflow isn't blocked, and avoid running an extension lead under a rug — plug it directly into a wall socket near where it will sit, the same rule that applies to any appliance left on overnight.

What noise level is quiet enough to sleep next to?

Under 30dB is genuinely unobtrusive, comparable to a quiet library; 30–35dB is the realistic range for most compact bedroom units and is fine for the majority of sleepers once the initial novelty wears off; above 38dB, expect it to be noticeable, especially the compressor's on-off click rather than the steady fan hum. Light sleepers should look specifically for a stated 'sleep mode' or 'night mode' that slows the fan and disables the click, even if it slightly reduces extraction rate overnight — a smaller drying effect you don't notice beats a bigger one that stops you sleeping.

Will a small dehumidifier stop condensation on bedroom windows?

It reduces it significantly but won't eliminate it entirely if the glazing itself is old single-pane, since the glass surface temperature is the real driver of condensation, not just room humidity. Running the unit through the evening and overnight, with the door closed so drier air doesn't just leak into the hallway, gives the best result. Pairing it with even a small gap of ventilation for ten minutes each morning to let the initial moisture-laden air out, then closing up and letting the dehumidifier take over, works better than running it against a fully sealed, unventilated room.

Can I use a dehumidifier in a rented apartment without a drain?

Yes — this is exactly what tank-based compact units are designed for, and it's the standard setup in the vast majority of Irish rentals. You simply empty the tank into a sink or bath when the indicator light shows full, typically every one to three days depending on how damp the room is. No drilling, no hose through a window, nothing that affects the deposit. If the tank fills unusually fast, that's useful information in itself — it means the room is more humid than expected and probably needs the vent or extractor fan used more, not just the dehumidifier.

Does a bedroom dehumidifier help with dust mites and allergies?

Indirectly, yes. Dust mites thrive above about 50% relative humidity and struggle to reproduce below it, so keeping a bedroom in the 40–50% range with a dehumidifier makes the environment measurably less hospitable to them over several weeks, which is the timescale that actually matters for allergy symptoms, not overnight. It won't remove mites or their droppings already in a mattress or carpet — that needs washing and vacuuming — but it stops the population growing back as quickly. A model with a HEPA stage built in adds some benefit by capturing airborne allergen particles as well.

How often will I need to empty a small dehumidifier's tank?

In a mildly humid bedroom, a 1–2L tank on a compact unit typically lasts two to three days before needing emptying. In a room with an active condensation problem — steamed-up windows most mornings — expect daily emptying, sometimes twice a day if you run the unit continuously rather than on a timer. Most units have a clear water-level window or an automatic shut-off light, so you're never guessing; if you find yourself emptying it more than once a day consistently, that's a sign the room needs a bigger-capacity unit or better ventilation alongside it.
