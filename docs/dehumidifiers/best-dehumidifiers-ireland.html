Buying guide: how to choose

What humidity level actually means you need a dehumidifier?

A €10 hygrometer from any hardware shop or Amazon.ie tells you your room's relative humidity in seconds, and that number matters more than any spec sheet. Irish indoor air comfortably sits at 40–50% RH; once a room holds above 60% for more than a day or two, mould has everything it needs. Target a machine with a humidistat that can be set to 50–55%. Most people who buy a dehumidifier are guessing — they bought because a window steamed up or a wardrobe smelled musty, not because they measured anything. Getting the reading first is what stops the unit churning away on a timer that has no idea what the room actually needs, costing money long after the air is dry.

How do you match dehumidifier capacity to your house?

Extraction figures on the box — 8L, 12L, 20L a day — are measured at 30°C and 80% RH in a lab, conditions an Irish home rarely reaches even in a wet November. Treat the number as a ceiling, not a promise. As a rough guide, a single bedroom or small flat is well served by 6–8L/day, a 3-bed semi with normal cooking and showering moisture needs 10–12L/day in one central spot or two smaller units, and a 4-bed house with a damp problem wants 20L/day or more. Undersizing is the most common mistake: a small unit in a big draughty house just runs constantly and never gets the air below its target, which wastes electricity without solving anything. If you're choosing between two similarly priced units, favour the one with a bigger tank and a humidistat range down to at least 35%, since it gives you room to correct for real-world extraction running lower than the box figure promises.

Does an old solid-wall house need a different dehumidifier than a new build?

A pre-1950s solid-wall cottage or terrace — no cavity, often lime mortar, walls that were built to breathe rather than seal — produces a different damp pattern than a 2005-onwards airtight build. Solid-wall homes lose heat fast and get cold internal surfaces, so moisture condenses on walls and window reveals even when air humidity looks moderate; the fix leans on extraction plus keeping background heat on low rather than letting rooms go fully cold. Newer, well-sealed houses trap moisture from cooking and showers because there's nowhere for it to escape without deliberate ventilation, so the dehumidifier is doing a job your PIV or extractor fan should share. Knowing which category your house falls into changes where you put the unit and how long you run it. A quick way to tell which you have: knock on an external wall — a hollow-ish, lighter knock usually means cavity construction, a dense, solid thud means solid wall.

What buying local instead of importing saves you

Amazon.ie, Currys, Harvey Norman, DID, Woodies and the brand's own Irish or EU store cover almost everything worth buying here. The saving from an obscure UK or US listing is rarely worth it once you account for a 20L machine's shipping weight, and warranty claims get complicated fast when the selling entity isn't registered in the EU — a two-year statutory warranty is far easier to enforce against an Irish or EU seller. Spare parts matter too: continuous-drain hoses, replacement filters and even tank lids vary by model, and a machine bought through a proper Irish retailer usually has parts available without a six-week wait. It's a dull thing to check before buying, but it's the difference between a five-minute fix and a dead dehumidifier in a drawer eight months later.

What does a dehumidifier cost to run over an Irish winter?

Do the sum before you buy, not after the first bill. A typical 12L compressor unit draws around 200W; run it 8 hours a day and that's 1.6kWh, which at the standard day rate of roughly 38 cent a unit works out to about €0.61 a day, or €18 across a 30-day month of steady use. Shift that same running time onto a smart-meter night rate — commonly 17–19 cent a unit — and the daily cost drops to around €0.29, so scheduling the unit to run overnight where the room allows it roughly halves the bill. Across a full damp season, October to April, that's the difference between €125 and €60 for the same dried-out house. A humidistat that cuts power once the room hits target does more for the annual bill than any efficiency claim on the box.

Frequently asked questions

How many litres per day dehumidifier do I need for a 3-bed semi in Ireland?

For one central unit covering hallway and landing airflow, aim for 10–12L/day extraction as a minimum, moving to 20L/day if the house has a persistent damp room or you want faster results without running the machine constantly. This is a lab-rated figure measured at 80% humidity, so real-world extraction in an Irish home at 55–65% RH will be noticeably lower — that's normal, not a fault. Two smaller units, one downstairs and one upstairs, often outperform a single large one because they don't have to fight the full volume of the house through hallway doors and stairwells.

Is it worth buying a dehumidifier with an air purifier built in?

Only if you'd genuinely buy the purifier separately anyway. Combined units are convenient and take up one socket instead of two, but the purifier stage is usually a basic HEPA filter, not a match for a dedicated unit, and it adds to the price. If your main problem is damp and mould spores together — a musty room with visible spotting — the combination earns its keep because it's actually filtering what the dehumidifier is drying out. If you just want dry air, skip the extra cost and put the difference toward a bigger tank or a hose-drain model instead.

Should I buy a dehumidifier online or from a shop like Currys or Harvey Norman?

Price is usually similar, so it comes down to what you value. Buying from Currys, Harvey Norman or a similar Irish retailer means an easy in-person return if the unit arrives faulty, and staff who can point you to a floor model to check the noise level yourself. Amazon.ie is faster and often cheaper on smaller brands, with the same two-year statutory warranty backing it as long as the seller ships from within the EU. For a large 20L machine that's awkward to return by post, buying in person carries a genuine practical advantage.

Do dehumidifiers work in an unheated Irish winter house?

Compressor models lose efficiency below roughly 15°C and can frost up their coils in a genuinely cold room, which is common in an Irish house with the heating off overnight or in a rarely-used spare room. Desiccant dehumidifiers don't have this problem — they use a moisture-absorbing wheel rather than a cold coil, so they keep pulling water out of the air at full rated capacity even at 5°C. If the room you're targeting regularly sits below about 15°C, a desiccant model will noticeably outperform a compressor one, even though it usually costs more to run per litre extracted.

How long does a dehumidifier last before the compressor gives out?

Manufacturer figures and owner reports across major brands point to a typical working life of 5–8 years for a compressor unit under normal domestic use, with desiccant models often lasting longer since there's no refrigerant compressor to fail, just a fan and a heating element. Continuous heavy use — running 24/7 in a genuinely wet house rather than the odd few hours — will shorten that. Keeping the filter clean and the air inlet free of dust does more for longevity than any other single habit, since a clogged filter forces the compressor to work harder for the same result.

What's the difference between a hygrometer reading and the dehumidifier's own display?

Built-in displays on cheaper units are often optimistic and can read 5–10 percentage points drier than the room actually is, because the sensor sits right next to the machine's own dry air outlet rather than in the room's general air. A separate €10 hygrometer placed a couple of metres away, at breathing height, gives a far more honest picture of what you're actually living in. If you're relying on the humidistat setting to cut the machine off automatically, it's worth cross-checking it against an independent hygrometer at least once to see how far out it runs.
