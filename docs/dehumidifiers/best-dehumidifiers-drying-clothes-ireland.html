Buying guide: how to choose

How much water does one wash load actually release into the air?

A full load spun at 1200–1400rpm still holds roughly 1.5–2.5 litres of residual water depending on fabric mix — towels and denim hold more, synthetics less. That water has to go somewhere: into a tumble dryer's exhaust or heat pump condenser, or into the room's air if you're drying on an airer, where a dehumidifier then has to pull it back out. A mid-size 8–12L/day unit comfortably handles one load's worth within a single overnight run. That's why laundry-mode dehumidifiers are sized the way they are — the extraction rate isn't really about the whole room, it's about matching what one wash load releases into the air over six to eight hours. It also explains why a single small load barely challenges a mid-size unit, while back-to-back laundry days can genuinely fill a tank or push a continuous-drain hose close to its working limit.

Laundry mode vs normal mode — what changes

Normal mode targets a humidity setpoint and cycles the compressor on and off once the room reaches it, which is efficient for general damp control but slow for wet clothes because the fan drops to low speed once the air feels dry, even though the clothes themselves are still holding water. Laundry mode overrides the humidistat and runs the fan at maximum continuously, prioritising airflow speed over the room's overall humidity reading — the goal shifts from 'dry air' to 'move as much air across wet fabric as possible.' It uses more electricity per hour than normal mode as a result, but finishes a load in roughly a third of the time, which is the actual point of the feature. Some models also raise the target temperature slightly in this mode, since warmer air holds more moisture and speeds up evaporation off the fabric, not just the extraction happening afterward.

Room setup: closed door, airer position, airflow path

A closed door matters more for drying speed than almost any other factor, because an open door lets the dehumidifier fight the volume of the whole house rather than one room. Position the airer so clothes face the machine's air outlet directly rather than side-on, and leave real gaps between items — a densely packed airer traps still, saturated air between garments that the machine's airflow never reaches. A small bathroom or box room with the door shut and the airer three-quarters of a metre from the unit will outperform the same machine in an open-plan kitchen-diner by a wide margin, purely because of enclosed volume and direct airflow. Tiled bathrooms and box rooms with hard, non-absorbent surfaces also dry faster than a carpeted spare room, since carpet and soft furnishings soak up ambient humidity and release it back slowly, which extends the time the dehumidifier needs to finish the job.

The real cost versus a tumbler, done properly

A typical vented or condenser tumble dryer costs roughly €1.30–1.80 per load at the standard 38 cent electricity rate, drawing 2–3kW for 60–90 minutes. A dehumidifier in laundry mode, drawing around 250–300W and running for 6–8 hours overnight to finish the same load, uses roughly 1.8–2.4kWh — about €0.68–0.91 a day on the standard rate, or closer to €0.35–0.45 on a smart-meter night rate. It's slower but meaningfully cheaper, and it does it without the fabric wear a tumble dryer's heat and tumbling action causes. Over a household doing four loads a week — about 17 loads a month — that works out at a saving of roughly €7–19 a month on the standard day rate, rising to €15–25 a month if you schedule the dehumidifier overnight on a smart-meter night rate. Factor in time as well as money: a tumble dryer finishes in under two hours, while the dehumidifier route takes most of a night, so it suits households that can plan a load the evening before rather than needing clothes dry within the hour.

Desiccant or compressor for a cold utility room?

Utility rooms and converted garages used for laundry are often the coldest, least-heated part of an Irish house, and that's precisely where compressor dehumidifiers underperform, losing extraction capacity as the room temperature drops and struggling below roughly 15°C. A desiccant model keeps close to its top extraction figure right down to around 5°C, which matters directly for laundry drying since a cold utility room with wet washing on an airer is exactly the scenario where a compressor unit would take noticeably longer than its rated figures suggest. The trade-off is a higher running cost per litre extracted and, on most desiccant models, a warmer exhaust air stream — which is actually useful in a laundry context, since it helps the fabric dry rather than just the room air. It's worth checking the model's stated minimum operating temperature specifically, since not every desiccant unit sold is rated as low as 5°C despite the technology generally coping better with cold than a compressor.

Frequently asked questions

How long does it take a dehumidifier to dry a load of washing indoors?

With a mid-size unit in laundry mode, a closed door, and a normally spun load, expect roughly 6–10 hours for full drying — an overnight run is the standard approach. Heavier items like towels, jeans and jumpers sit at the longer end; shirts and synthetics dry faster, sometimes in 4–5 hours. Running the same setup with the door open, or in a larger room, can roughly double that time because the machine is working against a bigger volume of air rather than a concentrated pocket of humidity around the airer.

Does drying clothes indoors with a dehumidifier cause mould?

No — that's specifically the problem a dehumidifier prevents. Drying clothes indoors without one is what causes mould, because the moisture released by wet washing has nowhere to go except into the room's air, walls and window reveals. A dehumidifier removes that same water as it evaporates off the clothes rather than letting it build up in the room, which is why laundry-mode dehumidifying is considered safer for indoor drying than an airer alone, provided the machine's tank or drain is keeping pace with what it's pulling out.

Is laundry mode different from normal dehumidifying mode?

Yes. Normal mode targets a set humidity level and slows or stops once the room reaches it, which is efficient for general damp control but leaves the fan running too slowly to dry wet fabric quickly. Laundry mode overrides that and runs the fan at maximum speed continuously regardless of the room's humidity reading, prioritising airflow across the clothes over overall room dryness. It uses more power per hour but finishes a load roughly three times faster, which is the entire reason the mode exists on models that offer it.

Can I dry clothes with a dehumidifier in a bedroom overnight?

It works, but a dedicated small room like a bathroom or box room is a better choice if one's available, since bedroom air quality and noise both matter more overnight than in an unoccupied room. If a bedroom is the only option, use a quieter compressor unit rather than a loud laundry-mode blast, accept a longer drying time, and make sure the room has some ventilation so you're not sleeping in air saturated with evaporating detergent and fabric-softener scent for eight hours.

Do I need a dehumidifier or just a clothes airer with a fan?

A fan alone speeds up evaporation off the clothes but does nothing about where that moisture ends up — it just spreads it around the room faster, which can make condensation on windows and cold walls worse, not better. A dehumidifier actually removes the water from the air rather than redistributing it. For occasional light drying a fan can shave some time off, but for regular indoor laundry drying through an Irish autumn and winter, the dehumidifier is doing a genuinely different job, not just a cheaper version of the same one.

Does a dehumidifier damage or shrink clothes the way a tumble dryer can?

No. A tumble dryer's shrinkage and wear come from heat and physical tumbling against a drum, neither of which a dehumidifier applies — clothes hang still on an airer while the machine simply removes moisture from the surrounding air. This makes dehumidifier drying gentler on wool, delicate synthetics and anything that's shrunk or pilled in a tumble dryer before. The trade-off is time and the lack of the fluffing effect tumbling gives towels, which come out drier but flatter than a tumble-dried equivalent.
