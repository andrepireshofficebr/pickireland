Buying guide: how to choose

What actually counts as a small garden for a robot mower in Ireland?

This covers the garden that's typical, not exceptional, for Ireland: an estate back garden roughly 80–250 m², often with a side passage, a shed, a small patio or decking area, and a boundary that isn't a clean rectangle. Irish planning guidance sets a bare minimum of 25 m² for rear private open space on new housing, and plenty of terraced and semi-detached properties sit not far above that floor. At this scale, the coverage figures on the box — often quoted up to 500 m² or more — are almost irrelevant. The real questions are whether the mower fits through your gate, turns inside a tight corner without repeated manoeuvring, and doesn't spend half its battery re-orienting around furniture in a garden a fraction of its rated capacity. Newer estates built to the current minimum tend to compress the garden further still once a shed, bin store and patio are accounted for.

Navigating tight, oddly shaped Irish gardens

Small Irish gardens are rarely open rectangles. A trampoline, a clothesline, raised vegetable beds, a child's playhouse and a narrow side return to the front garden are all common in the same 150 m² plot. Vision-based navigation, now standard on most small-garden robot mowers, handles this better than older bump-and-turn systems because the camera recognises an obstacle before contact and plots a path around it rather than reversing and retrying blindly. Narrow side passages — sometimes under a metre wide — are the hardest test: check the mower's stated minimum passage width against your own measurement before buying, since a model rated for a 60 cm gap on paper can still struggle if the passage has an uneven surface or a slight camber. It's also worth checking how the mower behaves at a dead end within a passage, since some models require enough space to turn rather than reverse back out the way they came in.

Noise, timing and terraced-house etiquette

Shared walls and gardens ten metres from a neighbour's bedroom window change what counts as an acceptable mowing schedule. Most small robot mowers run at 55–65 dB, quieter than a petrol mower but still audible at close range, so scheduling matters more than raw noise figures. Set cutting windows for mid-morning to early evening rather than very early or after dark, and check whether the app lets you define quiet hours the mower won't start during, which most models aimed at compact gardens now include. A mower that runs little and often — 20-minute sessions several times a week — is generally less disruptive to neighbours than one doing a long single weekly session, and it also suits Ireland's steady, low-intensity growth pattern better than infrequent heavy cutting. It's also worth checking the dock's own charging noise, which some owners find more noticeable indoors at night than the mower itself is during a daytime cutting session outside.

Don't pay for capacity you won't use

The single most common overspend on this page's shortlist is buying coverage you'll never need. A model rated for 500 m² carries a bigger battery, a wider cutting deck and often a higher price purely to sustain longer unattended runs — none of which helps a 120 m² garden finish any faster, since the limiting factor there is manoeuvring around obstacles, not raw battery endurance. For gardens under roughly 200 m², prioritise navigation precision, narrow-passage handling and app scheduling flexibility over headline coverage numbers, and treat any coverage figure more than double your actual lawn size as a sign you're paying for capacity that won't be used. Compare like-for-like by looking at the smallest model in a manufacturer's range rather than assuming a flagship simply scales down in price. A quick way to sanity-check this is to pace out your actual lawn area before shopping, since it's easy to overestimate a small garden's size by a third or more from memory alone.

Storage and charging in gardens without a shed

Not every small Irish garden has a shed to tuck a charging dock into, and the dock has to sit somewhere permanent, ideally with a clear run of a metre or two either side for the mower to dock accurately. Options include a small dedicated mower garage (a roofed box sized to the dock), a spot under an eave or overhang, or simply an exposed but weatherproof-rated position against a wall — most docks and mowers are rated for permanent outdoor placement. Whatever the location, keep it away from downpipes or spots where rainwater pools after heavy showers, since standing water around the charging contacts is a more realistic risk in Ireland than the rain itself. A short cable run from the nearest outdoor socket also matters at this scale, since extension leads left trailing across a small lawn are both a trip hazard and an obstacle the mower has to avoid.

Frequently asked questions

Is a robot mower worth it for a garden under 100 m²?

For most owners, yes, though the case is different than for a large lawn. At under 100 m², you're not buying a robot mower to save hours of mowing time — a garden that size takes ten minutes with a push mower. The appeal is consistency: short, frequent robotic cuts keep grass at an even height through Ireland's long growing season without you remembering to do it, and mulched clippings save the job of bagging and disposal. If convenience and a permanently tidy lawn matter more than the raw time saved, even a very small garden justifies an entry-level model.

Will a robot mower cope with a narrow side passage?

It depends on the passage width and the mower's turning geometry, not just its stated minimum gap. Measure the narrowest point of the passage, including any obstruction like a bin store or drainpipe, and compare it against the manufacturer's minimum passage width — most small-garden models handle 60–100 cm gaps, but tight 90-degree turns within that passage are harder than a straight run through it. Camera-based models generally cope better than older bump-sensor designs because they can line up the approach angle before entering the gap rather than nudging through by trial and error.

Can a small robot mower handle raised beds and garden furniture?

Static obstacles like raised beds, planters and garden furniture are exactly what modern obstacle-avoidance systems are designed for, and most small-garden robot mowers map them during the initial setup walk so they're avoided consistently rather than bumped into every pass. The harder case is furniture that moves — a table dragged out for summer, a paddling pool set up for a weekend — which the mower won't know about until it collides with it or, on camera-based models, detects it in real time and re-routes. If you rearrange the garden often, favour a model with real-time camera detection over one relying purely on a fixed map.

How do I stop a robot mower disturbing neighbours in a terraced estate?

Use the app's scheduling to keep mowing within a defined window — late morning to early evening covers most working patterns without running during typical nap times, early mornings or after dark. Position the charging dock away from a shared boundary wall if possible, since the mower is at its noisiest during the first metre of each pass leaving the dock. If a specific model's decibel rating isn't listed clearly, check owner reviews for mentions of noise, since real-world figures at the boundary can differ from the manufacturer's rating taken at the mower itself.

Do I need a charging shed for a small garden robot mower?

No — the charging dock and mower are both designed for permanent outdoor placement in Irish conditions, so a shed is a convenience rather than a requirement. It's worth having if you already have one, since it keeps the dock out of direct rain and makes the mower less visible from outside the garden, which has some anti-theft value. Where there's no shed, a small roofed mower garage or a sheltered spot against the house wall works just as well functionally, provided it's not somewhere rainwater collects after a downpour.
