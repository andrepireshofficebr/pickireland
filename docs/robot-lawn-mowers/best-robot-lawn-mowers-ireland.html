Buying guide: how to choose

Does garden shape matter more than garden size for a robot mower?

Two gardens with identical area can need completely different machines. A rectangular 300 m² lawn with one open boundary is an easy job for almost any robot mower; the same 300 m² split by a side passage, a raised bed, a shed and a washing line is a harder problem, because the machine has to navigate narrow gaps and avoid obstacles repeatedly rather than cut one big open rectangle. Before comparing coverage figures, sketch your own garden and count the pinch points: gates, narrow side returns, flower borders that cut across the lawn, trees with surface roots. A mower rated for double your actual area but with poor obstacle handling can still struggle in a fiddly Irish garden, while a lower-capacity model with tighter turning and camera-based obstacle detection may finish the same job faster and with fewer stuck-robot alerts.

Wire-free vs buried perimeter wire: the real decision

Every robot mower on this list falls into one of two camps: buried perimeter wire, or wire-free navigation using GPS-RTK, cameras, or both. Wire installation takes an afternoon of digging or pinning cable along every boundary, border and island you want protected, and any layout change — a new shed, an extended patio — means redoing sections of wire. Wire-free models skip the cable entirely; you walk the boundary once with a phone app and the mower remembers the route by satellite positioning or visual landmarks. The trade-off is upfront cost, since wire-free hardware still carries a premium, and RTK or camera systems can lose accuracy near tall hedges, close boundary walls or heavy tree cover. For most Irish gardens being set up from scratch in 2026, wire-free is now the sensible default; buried wire still suits very complex multi-zone rural sites where signal reliability is a concern.

How long is the grass-growing season in Ireland?

Grass in Ireland rarely stops growing outright — Teagasc's own grazing data shows the bulk of growth happens across spring, summer and early autumn, with only a genuine dormant period in mid-winter, and mild years can see soil temperatures creeping up again by late February. That long, low-intensity growing season is why robot mowers suit Irish lawns so well: instead of one demanding weekly cut, the mower runs short, frequent sessions that keep grass short year-round, mulching clippings back into the soil rather than leaving you with a bag to empty. When comparing coverage claims, check whether the manufacturer's figure assumes daily running through the growing months, because a mower rated for 500 m² on paper needs enough scheduled hours to keep up once growth accelerates in May and June.

Do robot mowers keep working in Irish rain and on slopes?

Ireland's rainfall is frequent rather than extreme, which matters for two reasons. First, most robot mowers on the market carry an IPX4-or-better rating, meaning they're built to keep working in drizzle and light showers rather than needing to be brought in at the first cloud. Second, a built-in rain sensor is worth prioritising over one without, since it pauses mowing during heavier downpours and resumes once the grass has dried enough to avoid rutting soft lawn or clogging the cutting deck with wet clippings. Slope handling is the other Irish-specific factor: gently sloped and uneven gardens are common outside flat estate plots, and manufacturer slope ratings (typically 20–35%) should be treated as a maximum, not a comfortable operating range, particularly on wet grass where wheel grip drops. On sites with heavier clay soil, several consecutive wet days can soften even a flat lawn enough to affect wheel grip, not just the sloped sections, so tyre or track design is worth a look if your garden drains slowly.

What separates a €500 robot mower from a €1,500 one?

At the low end, €500 buys reliable cutting for a modest, fairly flat, single-zone garden with basic app scheduling. Moving up the price ladder adds, in rough order: better obstacle avoidance through camera or lidar sensing rather than simple bump detection; multi-zone mapping so the mower can handle a front and back garden or a driveway split without manual intervention; all-wheel drive for sloped or rough rural ground; and RTK-grade positioning accurate to a few centimetres rather than tens of centimetres. At the top of the range, anti-theft features — PIN locks, GPS tracking, loud alarms — become standard, which matters because a robot mower left out on a lawn is a visible, portable target. Decide which of these you actually need for your garden before paying for the full set. Warranty length and Irish-based spare parts availability also tend to improve further up the range, which matters more once a machine is a few seasons old and needs a replacement blade disc or wheel.

Frequently asked questions

Can one robot mower cover a front and back garden separately?

Yes, provided the model supports multi-zone mapping, which most mid-range and premium wire-free mowers now do. During setup you map each area separately — front garden, back garden, side strip — and the mower either drives itself between zones along a marked passage or, if the areas aren't physically connected, you relocate it manually and select the correct zone in the app. Entry-level machines sometimes lack this feature and treat the whole property as one map, which causes confusion if the zones aren't connected by mowable grass. Check the specific model's zone limit before buying if your garden is split by a driveway, gate or path.

How long does first-time setup take for a wire-free model?

Budget half an afternoon for a typical Irish suburban garden. Most of that time goes into the boundary walk, where you guide the mower around the lawn's edge once so it can build a map using GPS-RTK, camera recognition, or both. Complex gardens with several zones, narrow passages or scattered obstacles take longer because the app usually asks you to walk each connecting route separately. After the initial map is saved, seasonal changes — new furniture, a different border — are minor edits, not a redo. Buried-wire installation, by comparison, typically takes a full day or more for the same garden.

What's the realistic lifespan of a robot lawn mower in Irish weather?

Most manufacturers design robot mowers for four to six years of seasonal use, and Irish conditions — frequent light rain rather than temperature extremes — are generally kinder to the electronics than climates with hard frost or intense summer heat. The parts that wear fastest are the cutting blades (replace every few months in a full growing season), the battery (typically rated for several hundred charge cycles before capacity drops noticeably), and the wheels on gardens with rough or muddy patches. Keeping the mower under cover between cuts and cleaning the underside of grass build-up after wet sessions both extend the practical lifespan well beyond the warranty period.

Do robot mowers need a garage or charging shelter?

Not strictly — most models are rated to sit outside on their charging dock permanently, with weatherproofing built in for Irish rain. That said, a simple roofed shelter or purpose-built garage extends component life by reducing direct rain and UV exposure on the housing and reduces the temptation for opportunistic theft, since a mower tucked under cover is less visible from the street. If you don't have a shed or side passage to site the dock, a basic mower garage — a small roofed box that fits over the charging station — is a low-cost addition worth considering for exposed gardens.

Can I switch from a wired to a wire-free robot mower without redoing the garden?

Yes — the old buried wire simply stays in the ground unused, or you can dig up sections you want to reclaim, but there's no requirement to remove it before installing a wire-free model. The new mower ignores the existing cable entirely and builds its own boundary map through the app walk-around. This is one of the most common upgrade paths in Ireland right now, since many gardens installed wired mowers years ago and are replacing an ageing unit with a wire-free model without wanting to dig up an established lawn to extract the cable.
