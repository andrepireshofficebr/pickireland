Buying guide: how to choose

What's the difference between a fixed-height and electric desk converter?

A standing desk converter sits on top of an existing desk and lifts a monitor and keyboard tray between sitting and standing heights, without replacing the desk itself. Spring-assisted or gas-lift converters use a counterbalance to make lifting one-handed and smooth, and are the most common type sold in Ireland; electric converters are less common and considerably pricier, mostly worth it if you're switching height multiple times a day and want single-button consistency. Negative-height converters that also drop the keyboard tray below desktop level suit taller desks where the fixed desk height already sits high for typing. The core trade-off versus a full standing desk is footprint: a converter sits on top of the existing surface, so it doesn't reclaim desk depth the way a slimmer standing desk frame might, and it adds height to the resting sitting position too.

How much weight can a standing desk converter actually hold?

Converters list a weight capacity that needs checking against the actual monitor-plus-keyboard-plus-accessories load, not just the monitor alone - a dual-monitor setup with a keyboard, mouse, and desk lamp on the raised tray can add up faster than expected, and most converters top out between 15-25kg. Desk depth compatibility is the other check that gets missed: a converter needs enough desk depth in front of it for the keyboard tray to sit at a comfortable typing distance even when the whole unit is pushed back against a monitor or wall, which on a desk under 60cm deep can leave uncomfortably little room. Measure the existing desk's depth and compare it against the converter's footprint plus a margin for elbow room before buying, rather than assuming any converter fits any desk.

Will a monitor arm fit your monitor and your desk?

Most monitors sold in the last decade support VESA mounting (a standard bolt pattern, usually 75x75mm or 100x100mm on the back of the screen), which is what lets a monitor arm hold and reposition it - check this before buying an arm, since a small number of budget or older monitors lack VESA holes entirely. Separately, check how the arm clamps to the desk: a desk-edge clamp needs a certain thickness and accessible edge, which some converters or thick standing desk tops don't leave room for, while a grommet-mount arm needs a drilled hole, awkward to retrofit on desks without one already. Weight range matters too - arms are rated for a specific monitor weight window, and an ultrawide or heavier monitor outside that range won't hold position reliably. Confirming the VESA pattern and monitor weight against the arm's spec before ordering avoids the common problem of a mount that technically fits but sags over time.

Do you need a footrest or an anti-fatigue mat?

A footrest and an anti-fatigue mat solve different postures and shouldn't be treated as interchangeable. A footrest addresses seated posture, giving shorter users or anyone whose desk height sits slightly high a surface to rest feet on rather than letting them dangle, which reduces pressure on the back of the thighs. An anti-fatigue mat addresses standing posture, cushioning the feet against a hard floor during standing desk use and reducing the fatigue that makes people abandon standing sessions early. Someone using a sit-stand setup regularly benefits from both, since they solve the comfort problem for each position rather than one covering both; someone who rarely stands can skip the mat entirely. Neither is expensive relative to the rest of a setup, which makes them an easy first upgrade to test before spending more on desk or converter hardware.

The real cost of a setup - and what Irish tax relief actually covers

A full sit-stand setup - converter or desk, monitor arm, footrest, mat - typically runs somewhere between €150 and €500 depending on how many pieces are new versus already owned, and it's worth knowing what Irish tax relief actually applies to before assuming any of it. Revenue's e-working relief and the related Remote Working Relief cover a portion of utility costs - electricity, heating and broadband - apportioned to days worked from home, and some employers pay a separate tax-free allowance toward those same costs; neither covers the purchase of a desk, converter or monitor arm itself. Equipment provided directly by an employer for work use is generally not treated as a taxable benefit, which is the more relevant route if a monitor arm or converter is genuinely work-required - worth raising with an employer before buying it personally.

Frequently asked questions

Is a standing desk converter as good as a full standing desk?

A converter gets most of the postural benefit of a full standing desk - the ability to alternate sitting and standing through the day - without the cost or the need to replace an existing desk, which makes it the more practical choice for renters or anyone not ready to commit to new furniture. What it doesn't match is stability at full height on cheaper models, and it adds bulk to the desk's sitting position since the whole unit sits on top of the existing surface. For someone testing whether standing desk use suits them before a bigger purchase, a converter is the lower-risk starting point.

Will a monitor arm work with a laptop instead of a monitor?

Yes, with a laptop stand or tray accessory rather than a direct monitor mount, since laptops don't have VESA mounting points on the back. Some monitor arms sell a laptop tray attachment that clips onto the same arm mechanism, letting a laptop sit at monitor height and angle rather than flat on the desk - useful for anyone using a laptop as their only screen. It won't hold the laptop as rigidly as a VESA-mounted monitor, and the laptop's own screen hinge angle still needs adjusting separately, but it solves the core height and viewing-angle problem.

Do I need a separate keyboard tray or can I type on the converter's main platform?

Most converters include a dedicated keyboard tray at a lower level than the main monitor platform, which is worth using rather than typing on the main surface - typing at monitor-shelf height, higher than a proper keyboard height, is a common cause of wrist strain on converters used without the tray. If a converter doesn't include a separate tray, that's worth treating as a real limitation rather than a minor omission, since keyboard height is one of the more consistently cited ergonomic factors in sit-stand research, alongside monitor eye-line height.

How much desk space does a converter actually take up when lowered?

Even lowered to sitting height, a converter typically adds 5-15cm of platform depth on top of the existing desk surface, plus whatever clearance the mechanism needs behind it to fold down fully - so a desk that felt adequately deep before a converter is added can feel noticeably tighter afterward. This matters most on desks already under 60cm deep, where a converter can leave very little room in front of the keyboard tray for wrists, a notepad, or mouse space. Measuring the lowered footprint, not just the raised one, avoids this surprising anyone after delivery.

Can my employer pay for or provide these accessories tax-free?

Equipment an employer buys and provides for genuine work use - a monitor arm, keyboard, converter, or full desk - is generally not treated as a taxable benefit-in-kind when it's used for work purposes, which makes raising it with an employer or IT/facilities team worth doing before buying personally, particularly for anyone in a hybrid role with a formal WFH arrangement. This is separate from Revenue's Remote Working Relief, which covers a portion of utility costs rather than equipment. Policies vary by employer - some run a fixed annual home office budget, others assess requests case by case - so it's worth asking directly rather than assuming either way.

What's the minimum accessory setup worth buying before a full desk upgrade?

A monitor arm and a decent chair upgrade generally deliver more comfort improvement per euro than a full desk replacement, since screen height and seated support affect posture continuously through the day, while desk surface size or standing capability matters less if the existing desk is merely unglamorous rather than genuinely too small or unstable. A footrest is the cheapest single addition worth considering if the desk sits even slightly too high. Reserve a full desk or converter purchase for when the existing surface is actually too small, unstable, or physically wrong height rather than just aesthetically dated.
