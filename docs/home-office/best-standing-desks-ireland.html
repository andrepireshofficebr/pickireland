Buying guide: how to choose

Single motor vs dual motor: what the difference actually buys you

Single-motor standing desks drive both legs from one motor through a synchronising bar, while dual-motor desks power each leg independently with its own motor, coordinated electronically. Dual motor is the meaningful upgrade for stability: it lifts more evenly under uneven loading (a monitor arm on one side, nothing on the other) and generally handles higher weight capacities, typically 100-125kg versus 70-80kg on single-motor frames. Dual motor desks also tend to reach full height faster and show less side-to-side twist during transition. Single-motor frames aren't bad - they suit lighter, more evenly distributed setups (laptop, single monitor) and cost meaningfully less - but anyone running dual monitors, a monitor arm, or planning to keep the desk a decade should treat dual motor as the baseline spec rather than the upgrade. Check the stated capacity against your own setup rather than assuming either type has headroom to spare.

Stability at height matters more than max height

Maximum height is the number every listing leads with, but wobble at height is what actually determines whether the desk feels usable standing. Wobble comes from frame geometry (wider leg stance and a crossbar or brace reduce it), leg thickness, and how far the desk is extended relative to its collapsed height - a desk near its max extension will always be less rigid than the same desk at mid-height. Manufacturer stability ratings are inconsistent across brands, so look for actual leg width, whether a stabiliser bar is included, and owner reports specifically mentioning typing stability at standing height rather than general reviews. A desk that reaches 125cm but shakes when typing is less useful day to day than one maxing at 118cm that stays rigid. If possible, ask a retailer directly whether a specific model has been tested for wobble at its top height rather than relying on the headline figure alone.

Weight capacity and what actually sits on a desk

Standing desk weight ratings quoted by manufacturers are usually measured as a static, evenly distributed load, not the dynamic combination most home offices actually run: two monitors on arms (12-20kg for the arm hardware alone before the screens), a laptop dock, a desk lamp, books, and the habit of leaning on the edge while adjusting a chair. Add these up before assuming a 70kg-rated desk has comfortable headroom, particularly with monitor arms, which concentrate weight at a single clamp point rather than spreading it across the desktop. A reasonable rule is to keep actual load under 60-70% of the rated maximum, leaving margin for the desk's own weight-lifting mechanics to work smoothly rather than straining near its ceiling every day. Desks used for dual-monitor setups with arms are the ones most likely to exceed a conservative estimate, so weighing the full load once assembled is a useful sanity check.

Memory presets and controller quality

Height-adjustable desks with digital controllers let you save preset heights - typically two to four - so switching between sitting and standing is one button press rather than holding a control while watching a number climb. This matters more than it sounds for whether the desk actually gets used for its intended purpose: friction in switching height is the main reason sit-stand desks end up parked in one position permanently. Better controllers also show height in centimetres rather than an arbitrary scale, which helps when setting up monitor eye-line, and some include anti-collision sensors that stop the desk if it meets resistance while rising - worth having if the desk sits near a windowsill, shelf or low ceiling in a box room. A desk without presets still works, but every height change becomes a small daily friction point that adds up over months of use.

Getting a heavy desk frame up an Irish staircase

Desk frames typically ship in two or three boxes weighing 20-30kg each, and Irish terraced and semi-detached houses with narrow staircases or tight turns can make the second-floor box-room installation genuinely awkward - measure the stairwell width and any turn radius before ordering a desk destined for an upstairs office. Most retailers deliver to the door, not the room, so factor in carrying two heavy boxes up stairs alone or arranging help. Assembly generally takes 45-90 minutes with two people, mostly because aligning the motorised legs to the desktop needs care. A small number of Irish retailers offer paid room-of-choice delivery and assembly, worth the extra cost for a heavy frame going upstairs. Where access is genuinely tight, some buyers choose to assemble the desk in the room it'll live in rather than carrying a built unit up the stairs at all.

Frequently asked questions

How much weight can a standing desk actually put on an upstairs floor?

A standing desk plus a full monitor setup typically weighs 40-60kg total, well within what any normal domestic floor - timber joist or concrete - is built to handle; floor loading only becomes a real concern with genuinely heavy equipment like a safe or a full bookcase of hardback books, not office furniture. The more relevant upstairs consideration in older Irish houses is floor flex rather than load limit: some timber floors have slight give underfoot, which can telegraph as a faint bounce through a tall desk at standing height. It's rarely a stability problem, more a sensation worth being aware of on older properties.

Do standing desks work on uneven or older floors?

Most standing desks have independently adjustable foot levellers on each leg, which handle the kind of unevenness common in older Irish homes - slightly sloped floors, uneven floorboards, or a few millimetres of variance between corners of a room. Levellers typically adjust a centimetre or two per leg, enough for normal domestic floor variance but not for a significantly sloped attic conversion or very uneven original floorboards. If the room has a noticeable slope, check the leveller range specifically before buying, and consider a rubber levelling shim as backup - a rocking desk at standing height is more noticeable and more annoying than a rocking desk while seated.

How often should I actually switch between sitting and standing?

There's no universally agreed number, but ergonomic guidance generally suggests alternating every 30-60 minutes rather than standing for hours at a stretch, since prolonged standing brings its own fatigue and lower-back load. A common practical pattern is standing for meetings and calls, sitting for focused typing work, which naturally breaks up the day without needing a timer. The point of a sit-stand desk is reducing total sedentary time and enabling movement, not maximising standing time specifically - standing all day with poor posture isn't meaningfully better than sitting all day with poor posture. Presets that make switching a single button press matter more than any specific ratio.

What's the realistic lifespan of a standing desk motor?

Standing desk motors are generally rated for tens of thousands of height adjustment cycles, which for typical use - two to four height changes a day - translates to well over a decade before motor wear becomes likely. Manufacturer warranties reflect this confidence: motor and frame warranties of 5-10 years are common even on mid-priced desks, notably longer than the warranty on the desktop surface itself, which is more prone to cosmetic wear. The more common failure mode reported by owners isn't motor burnout but controller electronics or connection cables, particularly on desks left in a cold, damp room such as an unheated garage conversion.

Do I need an anti-fatigue mat with a standing desk?

An anti-fatigue mat isn't essential but it addresses a real and specific problem: standing on a hard floor (tile, laminate, concrete) concentrates pressure through the feet in a way carpet or an area rug doesn't, and that pressure is what causes standing fatigue faster than the standing itself. If the desk sits on carpet, a mat adds comfort but solves less. If it sits on hardwood, laminate or tile - common in Irish kitchen-extension or converted-garage home offices - a mat meaningfully extends how long standing feels comfortable and is one of the cheaper upgrades available.

Can a standing desk be used with an existing desktop I already own?

Standing desk converters (risers that sit on top of an existing desk) are built exactly for this and need no permanent desk replacement, while full standing desk frames are usually sold as base-only, meaning any flat desktop of suitable size can be attached with the included brackets - useful if you already own a desktop you like. Check the frame's desktop size range (both minimum and maximum length/depth) against your existing surface before buying, since narrow frames won't support a wide desktop safely, and confirm the desktop thickness fits the mounting hardware, typically 2.5-4cm.
