Buying guide: how to choose

Why does an electric scooter's weight matter so much in Irish housing?

A lot of Irish housing simply doesn't have anywhere to leave a scooter outside — no shed, no hallway big enough, an apartment block with stairs and no lift, or a terraced house where the only storage is behind the front door. That makes carrying weight a daily, not occasional, consideration: the difference between a 13 kg scooter and a 19 kg one is the difference between lifting it one-handed up a flight of stairs and needing both arms and a rest halfway up. Manufacturer weight figures are usually accurate since they're a simple, verifiable spec, unlike range claims — but check whether the quoted weight includes the battery, since some listings quote the lighter unfolded frame weight separately. Even a few extra kilograms compounds quickly if you're also carrying groceries, a work bag or a child's schoolbag on the same trip up the stairs.

Can you take an e-scooter on public transport in Ireland?

It's worth being clear about this because it changes what 'lightweight' is actually for: e-scooters have been banned from all National Transport Authority services since late 2024, covering Dublin Bus, Bus Éireann, Go-Ahead Ireland, Iarnród Éireann, Luas and TFI Local Link, because of the fire risk from lithium-ion batteries in an enclosed vehicle. So a folding scooter won't get you onto the DART or Luas the way a folding bike sometimes can — the lightness is for carrying it up your own stairs, into a car boot, or through an office lobby, not for boarding a train. If your commute plan depends on scooter-then-train, that plan needs a rethink; if it's scooter-to-door with some stair-carrying involved, lightweight genuinely earns its keep. Some riders assume the ban only applies to larger scooters or peak-hour services, but it's a flat, year-round rule with no size exception written into it anywhere.

What do you give up buying a lighter e-scooter?

Lighter scooters generally mean smaller wheels, a smaller battery and a lighter frame, and each of those is a trade-off. Smaller wheels, often 7-8.5 inches, catch on potholes and cobbled surfaces more readily than 10-11 inch wheels, since a smaller wheel has to climb a proportionally bigger obstacle relative to its own size. A smaller battery means less range, so the same 'lightweight commuter' badge that makes a scooter easy to carry usually also means charging more often. None of this makes a lightweight scooter a worse choice — it's the right trade if your route is short and mostly smooth, and the wrong one if it's long or rough. Weigh this against your actual route before deciding: a short, flat, well-surfaced commute barely notices the difference, while a longer or rougher one will make the range and wheel-size trade-offs obvious within the first week.

What makes a good folding mechanism on an e-scooter?

Not all folds are equal. A single-lever fold that locks with an audible click is faster and safer to trust daily than a multi-step mechanism with a separate pin or clip that's easy to leave half-engaged. Check owner reports for mentions of the fold loosening over months of daily use — this is one of the most common wear points on budget folding scooters, since the hinge takes real mechanical stress every single time you fold and unfold it. A scooter that folds well but is awkward to carry once folded, with no shoulder strap or an unbalanced centre of gravity, still isn't a good daily-carry choice even at a low weight. It's worth folding and unfolding a candidate scooter several times in the shop before buying, since a stiff or notchy mechanism when new rarely improves with age.

Does a lightweight scooter still need weatherproofing?

Folding a scooter away doesn't reduce how much rain it rides through on the way there — an IP54 rating or better still matters exactly as much on a lightweight model as a heavy one, and lightweight scooters don't get a pass on this just because they're compact. If anything, budget lightweight models sometimes skip a stated water rating to save cost and weight, so check the spec explicitly rather than assuming a smaller scooter is inherently more weather-sealed. Wipe the scooter down before folding it against clothing or bedding indoors, since a wet fold point left closed is exactly the kind of damp, enclosed space where corrosion starts. A scooter left folded in a damp hallway for weeks between rides benefits from an occasional check of the hinge and charging port for any trapped moisture or corrosion starting to form.

Frequently asked questions

Can I bring a folded e-scooter on the Luas or DART?

No. E-scooters, folded or not, have been banned from all National Transport Authority services since late 2024 — this covers Dublin Bus, Bus Éireann, Go-Ahead Ireland, Iarnród Éireann, Luas and TFI Local Link — because of the fire risk lithium-ion batteries pose in an enclosed passenger vehicle. This is a blanket ban regardless of the scooter's size, weight or whether it's switched off, so a lightweight folding model doesn't get an exception. Plan your route as scooter-to-door, not scooter-plus-train.

What's a realistic weight for carrying a scooter up stairs daily?

Most people can comfortably carry 12-14 kg up a flight of stairs one-handed without much strain; above 17-18 kg it typically needs both hands and becomes a two-trip job if you're also carrying a bag. If your daily route includes stairs with no lift, prioritise scooters under 15 kg specifically, even if it means accepting a shorter claimed range — you'll feel the weight difference within the first week far more than you'll feel a few kilometres of missing range.

Do lightweight scooters wear out faster than heavier ones?

Not inherently, but the fold hinge on any lightweight folding scooter takes more mechanical stress relative to the frame's overall size than on a bulkier, non-folding model, simply because it's folded and unfolded daily. Owner reports across budget folding models commonly mention the fold mechanism loosening before other components fail. A well-made hinge with a firm locking click tends to hold up better over daily use than a cheaper pin-and-clip design, regardless of the scooter's overall weight.

Are small wheels a problem on Dublin's cobbled streets?

Yes, more than on smooth tarmac. Smaller wheels, common on lightweight folding scooters, have to ride over the same size cobble or setted stone as a larger wheel would, but the obstacle is proportionally bigger relative to a small wheel's diameter, producing a rougher, less stable ride. If your regular route crosses cobbled or setted areas — common around older Irish town and city centres — a lightweight scooter with at least 8.5 inch wheels rides noticeably better than one with 7 inch wheels.

Is a folding scooter road-legal in the same way as a full-size one?

Yes, the legal requirements don't change based on size or whether a scooter folds. It still needs to meet the same 400 W continuous power cap, 20 km/h design speed, 25 kg maximum weight and 200 mm minimum wheel diameter, along with lights, brakes, a bell and reflectors, to be legal on Irish roads. Being small and light doesn't exempt a scooter from any part of the technical spec — check the manufacturer's plate the same way you would on a bigger model.

Is it safe to store an e-scooter battery indoors?

Generally yes, if you follow basic lithium-ion battery precautions: charge on a hard, non-flammable surface, don't leave it charging unattended overnight in a small bedroom, avoid charging immediately after riding in the rain until it's dried off, and don't store it somewhere it could be damaged or punctured. This matters more for apartment dwellers storing a scooter in a hallway or small flat than for someone with a shed, simply because there's less separation between the battery and where people are sleeping.
