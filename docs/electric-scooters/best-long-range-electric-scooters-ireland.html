Buying guide: how to choose

Why does an e-scooter's real-world range fall short of the claimed figure?

Manufacturer range figures come from controlled test conditions: a light rider, flat ground, a steady low speed and a fully charged, brand-new battery. Between hills, wind off the Atlantic or the Irish Sea, a rider closer to 80–90 kg, stop-start city traffic and a battery a year into its life, real-world range typically lands at 60–70% of the claimed figure. A scooter advertised at 60 km should realistically be planned around 36–42 km. For a 15 km each-way commute, that means the 60 km claim is close to the minimum you'd want, not a comfortable margin — buy toward the higher end of claimed range if your round trip is anything but short and flat. Treat any listing that doesn't mention rider weight or terrain in its range claim with a healthy dose of caution.

Battery size, explained without the marketing

Range comes down to watt-hours (Wh), which is voltage multiplied by amp-hours — a 48V battery rated at 15Ah stores roughly 720 Wh, noticeably more than a 36V 10Ah pack at around 360 Wh, even though both numbers look similarly modest on a spec sheet. Bigger batteries add weight, which is the trade-off for range: a long-range scooter is rarely the lightest one to carry upstairs. When comparing listings, convert to Wh rather than trusting the advertised km figure alone, since two scooters claiming '60 km range' can have genuinely different battery capacities depending on how optimistically each brand tested that number. A quick way to sanity-check a listing: divide the claimed range by the battery's Wh figure — scooters clustering around a similar efficiency per Wh are being honest, while outliers claiming unusually high range per Wh are worth treating with scepticism.

Hills eat range faster than distance does

A flat 10 km ride and a 10 km ride with sustained climbs — think Cork's northside hills or Dublin routes through Rathfarnham or Killiney — can draw noticeably different amounts of battery, even though the legal top speed is capped at 20 km/h either way. Climbing draws far more current than cruising on the flat, so a commute with real elevation change should be planned around the lower end of the realistic range estimate, not the claimed figure. This is also where continuous motor power (up to the legal 400 W limit) matters for range as much as for speed: a motor working near its limit on every hill drains the battery faster than one with headroom to spare. If your commute has one or two steep sections rather than constant gradient, that's usually enough on its own to push real range toward the lower end of what you'd otherwise expect on mostly flat ground.

Comfort features that matter over 10km+

Longer rides change what's worth paying for. Front suspension or a suspension-tuned deck reduces fatigue on longer commutes and on Ireland's less-than-perfect road surfaces, and a wider deck lets you shift foot position on a 20-30 minute ride the way you can't on a narrow board built for short hops. Larger-diameter tyres, 10 inches or more, roll over drain covers, potholes and tram tracks with less jolt than smaller wheels, which matters more the longer you're standing on the deck. None of these features change the legal 20 km/h cap, but they change how tired you are after riding it every day. None of these upgrades are visible in a product photo, so check the spec sheet for suspension type and tyre diameter specifically rather than assuming a premium-looking scooter includes them as standard.

What it costs to charge a bigger battery

At Ireland's roughly €0.38 per kWh domestic rate, a large 700 Wh battery costs around 25-27 cents for a full charge from empty, compared to roughly 13-15 cents for a smaller 350-400 Wh pack — still a rounding error next to a monthly Leap card or fuel spend. Charging a bigger battery from a standard 3-pin socket typically takes longer, often 8-10 hours for a full-capacity pack, so long-range scooters suit overnight charging routines better than a quick top-up between short trips. The extra range costs almost nothing in electricity; the real cost is the higher purchase price and the extra weight you're carrying. Even a heavy daily user charging a large battery every night rarely spends more than €8-10 a month on electricity — a fraction of a single tank of petrol or a weekly bus pass.

Frequently asked questions

How much claimed range do I actually need for a 15 km commute?

Plan around 60-70% of the claimed figure holding up in real conditions, so for a 15 km each-way (30 km round trip) commute, look for a claimed range of at least 45-50 km, and closer to 60 km if your route includes hills or you're a heavier rider. That leaves a buffer for cold mornings, headwinds and a battery that's a year or two into its life rather than fresh out of the box, and avoids the stress of watching the battery indicator on the ride home.

Does cold Irish winter weather reduce e-scooter range?

Yes. Lithium-ion batteries lose a meaningful amount of usable capacity in cold temperatures, and Irish winter mornings around freezing can noticeably shorten range compared with the same battery in summer. It's temporary — capacity returns once the battery warms up during use or is stored somewhere above freezing — but if you're commuting on frosty mornings, expect a bit less range than your summer rides and don't read a colder-weather trip as a sign the battery is failing.

Is a bigger battery worth the extra weight for portability?

It depends on your route. If you're riding door to door without carrying the scooter, extra weight barely matters and the range is worth it. If you're lifting it up stairs, over a step onto a bus platform, or into a car boot daily, an extra 3-5 kg from a bigger battery is noticeable every single day, while the range benefit only matters on your longest rides. Long-range and lightweight are genuinely different priorities — decide which one your actual commute needs more.

Do long-range scooters cost more to insure in Ireland?

Compliant e-scooters don't require compulsory insurance regardless of battery size or range, so there's no mandatory cost difference. If you choose to add optional third-party liability cover through a home insurance policy, pricing is generally based on the rider and use case rather than the specific battery capacity, so a long-range model shouldn't cost meaningfully more to cover than a shorter-range one. If you're shopping around, ask insurers directly rather than assuming, since e-scooter add-on cover is still a relatively new product in the Irish market and policies vary in what they include.

Can a powerful long-range scooter go faster than 20 km/h?

The motor might be capable of it, but the design speed on a compliant Irish e-scooter is legally capped at 20 km/h regardless of battery size or motor power. A bigger battery and stronger motor improve how well the scooter holds that speed on hills and under load, not how fast it's allowed to go. Riding above 20 km/h on a public road is a fixed charge offence whether the extra speed comes from a big battery or a small one.

How often should I recharge a long-range battery to protect its lifespan?

Lithium batteries generally last longest when kept roughly between 20% and 80% charge for daily use, rather than routinely running to empty or sitting at 100% for long periods. With a bigger battery you have more flexibility to top up little and often without needing a full overnight charge every time, which can extend the number of years before you notice real range loss compared with regularly draining a smaller pack to zero.
