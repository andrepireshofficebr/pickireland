Buying guide: how to choose

How do you size an air purifier for an open-plan Irish home?

Measure the whole connected space, not just the part you sit in: a 45 m² kitchen-dining-living run with a 2.4 m ceiling is 108 m³, and a double-height void or open stairs can push that to 140–160 m³. Three air changes an hour at 108 m³ needs 324 m³/h of clean air delivery — five changes needs close to 550. Open-plan is the default in Irish builds from the 2000s onward, which is exactly why the sizing rules printed on purifier boxes mislead here. Very few single domestic machines produce that kind of clean-air delivery at a noise level anyone would tolerate while watching television. This calculation is what decides whether you buy one flagship, two mid-size units, or consciously accept two air changes an hour as good enough for the space.

What CADR do you need at a usable, quiet fan speed?

Headline CADR is measured at maximum fan speed, and on large machines maximum is typically 55–65 dB — louder than conversation. The figure to hunt for is delivery at the second or third of five speeds, which is where a big unit actually lives day to day. As a working rule for Irish open-plan rooms: under 80 m³, look for a 300 m³/h headline; 80–120 m³, aim at 400 m³/h; above 120 m³, either 600 m³/h or two machines. Expect roughly half the headline number at a comfortable speed and size up accordingly — a unit rated 500 m³/h running at 250 in the evening still gives two-plus changes an hour in a 108 m³ room without anyone raising their voice. Oversizing is the only reliable route to quiet in a large space.

One large unit or two smaller ones

Two mid-size purifiers usually beat one large one in an open-plan room, for a reason unconnected to total airflow. Air does not mix instantly. A single machine at one end creates a clean zone around itself while the far corner — behind the island, past the sofa — stays comparatively dirty, because furniture, peninsulas and changes in ceiling height all create dead air. A pair placed at opposite ends covers the volume far more evenly, lets both run at lower and quieter speeds, and means one failure does not leave you with nothing. The counter-argument is consumables: two sets of filters every year instead of one. Price both scenarios across five years, because two €180 units with €35 filters frequently land cheaper than one €450 flagship with €80 filters.

Cooking, stoves and the evening spike

The largest particle event in an Irish open-plan house happens between six and eight in the evening. Frying, grilling and toasting throw PM2.5 into the room in quantities that dwarf anything drifting in from outdoors, and in an open-plan layout there is no door to contain it. A stove or open fire in the same connected space adds a further load every time the door is opened to refuel. Two implications for choosing a machine. Put real weight on carbon capacity — a substantial carbon layer, measured in hundreds of grams, deals with cooking odours and smoke gases that HEPA media ignores entirely. And favour a responsive particle sensor with fast auto reaction, because you want full speed within a minute of the pan going on, not twenty minutes later.

How much does running a large air purifier cost per year in Ireland?

Large purifiers consume real electricity. A high-CADR unit averaging 45 W across the day uses 394 kWh a year, roughly €150 at a 38 cent unit rate — not trivial. Run it hard at 70 W and you are past €230. Filters follow the same curve: large-format HEPA-and-carbon cartridges cost €60–100, and heavy carbon loading in a cooking space usually means annual rather than eighteen-monthly replacement. A realistic five-year total for a €400 machine is around €400 in filters plus €600–750 in electricity, which makes the appliance itself the smallest line on the bill. Two levers pull that down: use auto mode so the unit idles at 10–15 W between events, and check the wattage at the speeds you will genuinely use rather than the maximum figure stamped on the plate.

Frequently asked questions

Will one air purifier clean a whole house?

No. Purifiers clean the room they occupy, and the effect falls away sharply past a doorway — a closed door blocks it almost entirely, and even an open one leaves the next room receiving a small fraction of the clean air delivery. Manufacturer "whole home" claims generally assume a single air change per hour across an unrealistically open floor plan. In practice a two-storey Irish semi needs one unit downstairs in the main living space and another in the principal bedroom before you can reasonably say the house is covered.

Where should a purifier go in an open-plan kitchen-living room?

Between the two zones rather than in either of them, and out of the corners. The classic mistake is tucking it behind the sofa at the far end, where it cleans a bubble around itself while cooking particles fill the other half of the room. A position along the main run, with clear space on all sides and near the path air naturally takes between the hob and the seating area, does far more work. If you can only choose one end, choose the one nearer the cooker — that is where the biggest particle events originate.

Does a good extractor hood mean I don't need a purifier in the kitchen?

A ducted extractor vented outside is the better tool for cooking particles, and if you have one, use it — switch it on before the pan heats and leave it running fifteen minutes afterwards. Many Irish kitchens, though, have recirculating hoods that trap grease and vent straight back into the room, which does very little for PM2.5. A purifier is a useful backstop either way, because an open-plan layout lets whatever escapes the hood spread across the whole ground floor instead of staying in one room.

Can an air purifier cope with a stove or open fire in the same room?

It will help with what escapes into the room — particularly the puff of smoke and fine particulate released each time the door is opened to refuel, and the general PM2.5 rise on a still evening. It is not a fix for a badly drawing chimney or a poorly sealed stove, both of which need a sweep or an installer. Prioritise carbon capacity if this describes your sitting room, since HEPA media catches soot particles but none of the gases, and expect noticeably shorter filter life across the heating season.

Should the unit sit on the floor or on furniture?

It depends on where it breathes. Units with a base or lower-side intake and a top outlet work well on the floor, which is also where much of the heavier dust settles. Models drawing in from the front at mid-height do better raised 30–60 cm on a low table or console. Either way the priority is clearance at the intake faces. In a large room a slightly raised position also helps the clean-air stream travel further before it mixes, which matters when one machine is trying to cover 100 m².

Is a big purifier on a low speed better than a small one on high?

Almost always, for the same delivered airflow. A large impeller turning slowly moves air with less turbulence and less noise than a small one spinning hard, so the bigger machine gives equivalent cleaning at a level you can talk over. It also carries more filter area, which means lower resistance, lower power draw per cubic metre moved, and longer intervals between replacements. The trade-offs are purchase price, floor space and a heftier filter bill when it finally comes due. In an open-plan room, buying a size up is rarely a mistake.
