Buying guide: how to choose

What does a 25 dB air purifier actually sound like?

Decibels are logarithmic, so the gap between 25 dB and 35 dB is far wider than it reads — every 10 dB is roughly a doubling of perceived loudness. A quiet Irish bedroom sits around 20-30 dB of background noise at night, so a purifier at 24 dB effectively disappears into it, while one at 38 dB is impossible to ignore at 3am. The catch is measurement. Manufacturer figures are taken in an anechoic chamber at one metre; the same unit on a hard bedside locker 40 cm from your ear, reflecting off a wall, reads higher in practice. Treat quoted sleep-mode numbers as a ranking tool between models rather than a promise. Any figure given without a distance, or as an "as low as" range with no speed named, is not really a specification.

Sleep-mode CADR: the number that decides everything

Any purifier can be silent — switch it off. What matters is how much clean air survives the quiet setting. Brands headline maximum CADR at top speed, which on most machines is 50–55 dB and unusable in an occupied bedroom. The useful question is airflow at the lowest setting. A unit delivering 60–80 m³/h below 25 dB turns over a 29 m³ Irish double bedroom two to three times an hour, which is enough to matter. One that drops to 25 m³/h in order to hit its silence claim gives under one change an hour and is essentially decorative. Where brands publish per-speed tables, read them. Where they do not, owner reports consistently mention whether the low setting moves perceptible air at arm's length, and that crude proxy is better than nothing.

Big fan turning slowly beats small fan turning fast

Noise comes from air velocity and turbulence, not from cleaning. A 360 mm cylindrical unit with a large impeller moves the same volume as a compact tower at a far lower blade speed, and lower blade speed means less broadband hiss. That is why physically larger purifiers tend to be the quiet ones, and why an oversized machine loafing on setting one beats a small one straining on setting three. Motor type matters as well: brushless DC motors run smoother and allow genuinely low speeds, while cheap AC motors have a floor below which they stall or hum. The last variable is tone. Broadband whoosh disappears into sleep; a bearing whine, or a resonance that appears at one particular speed, does not — and it is the most common complaint in long-term owner feedback.

Light, draught and the three-in-the-morning problems

Two non-acoustic details ruin bedroom purifiers. The first is light: air-quality LEDs, illuminated rings and bright displays are genuinely disruptive in a dark room, so the unit needs a full display-off mode rather than a dimmed one — a strip of tape is a poor answer on a €300 appliance. The second is auto mode. A purifier left on auto ramps up whenever the sensor sees a spike, and in a bedroom that spike is usually you rolling over and disturbing dust. Waking at three to a machine at full tilt defeats the point, so favour models whose sleep mode locks the fan speed instead of merely dimming the lights. Draught is the third consideration: a directional tower pointed at the bed is unpleasantly cold in an Irish bedroom in January.

Overnight running costs and how fast the filter loads

A bedroom-only purifier is cheap to run. At 6 W on sleep mode for eight hours a night, a full year of overnight use is 17.5 kWh — about €7 at current Irish unit rates. Leave the same unit on low around the clock and it is roughly 53 kWh, or €20 a year. Neither figure should change your decision; filters will. Night-only running loads the media far more slowly than continuous operation, so an element rated for twelve months of constant use often stretches well past that on a bedroom duty cycle. Judge replacement by airflow and smell rather than by the counter, which on most machines simply counts hours regardless of load. Budget €30–60 a year and check that price before buying, because small units are where filter mark-ups are steepest.

Frequently asked questions

How many decibels is too loud for a bedroom?

Above about 30 dB most people notice a purifier in a quiet room, and above 35 dB it becomes the loudest thing in there. A typical Irish bedroom at night has a background of 20–30 dB, so anything at or below 25 dB effectively disappears into it. Remember that manufacturer figures are measured at one metre in a treated room; on a hard bedside locker close to your head, add a few decibels in practice. If you are a light sleeper, prioritise models that publish a dB(A) figure for each fan speed over those quoting a single "as low as" number with no context.

Is the white noise from a purifier good for sleep?

For some people, genuinely yes — a steady broadband hiss masks traffic, wind and a partner's movement, and plenty of owners keep a unit running mainly for that. What matters is the character of the sound rather than its level. Smooth, low-frequency airflow noise is easy to sleep through; a bearing whine, a motor hum at one particular speed, or a fan that surges up and down on auto is not, and habituation does not fix it. If you want the masking effect, choose a unit that holds a fixed medium speed rather than one that hunts.

Should I use auto mode or sleep mode overnight?

Sleep mode, in most bedrooms. Auto reacts to the sensor, and the sensor reacts to ordinary night-time events — turning over, the duvet moving, dust stirred as you get up. The result is a machine ramping to a noisy speed at three in the morning, which is the single most common complaint in long-term owner feedback. A fixed low speed cleans a bedroom-sized volume perfectly well across eight hours. Save auto mode for daytime use in living areas, where a spike from cooking or an opened door is genuinely worth an immediate response.

Can I put the purifier in a corner or under the bed?

No to under the bed, and corners are a compromise. Almost all purifiers draw air in through the sides or base and exhaust upwards or forwards, so boxing one in starves the intake, drops actual delivery well below the rated CADR, and makes the motor work harder and louder for less clean air. Aim for 30 cm of clear space around the intake faces, and avoid deep pile carpet if the unit breathes from underneath. Where floor space is tight, a tall slim tower against a wall loses less performance than a cylindrical model jammed into a corner.

Do purifiers make a bedroom colder or draughty?

They do not cool the air — the motor actually adds a watt or two of heat — but they move it, and moving air feels colder against skin. In an Irish bedroom in winter, with an external wall sitting at 14–16 °C, an outlet pointed at the bed is noticeably chilly. The fix is placement rather than a different machine: angle the discharge along the room or up towards the ceiling, or choose a cylindrical model with 360-degree output that never concentrates flow in one direction. On sleep mode the airflow is usually too gentle to feel at all.

Do air purifiers get louder as the filter clogs?

Usually yes, and it is a useful signal. As media loads with dust, resistance rises, and a fan holding the same speed against more back-pressure gets louder while moving less air. On units with fixed manual speeds you hear it first as a change in tone; on models with automatic pressure compensation you may only see the power draw creep up. Vacuuming or rinsing the pre-filter monthly delays it considerably. If a machine that used to disappear at night is now audible on the same setting, check the pre-filter before assuming the main element is finished.
