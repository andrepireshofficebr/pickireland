Buying guide: how to choose

What are Irish air purifiers actually filtering out?

Irish indoor air is dominated by year-round mould spores (2–10 microns) from persistent humidity, large grass pollen (20–35 microns) that any decent filter traps easily, and sub-micron combustion particles from open fires and stoves that no fibre filter touches alone. That mix calls for a true H13 filter rated to 0.3 microns plus genuine activated carbon measured by weight, not a thin mesh sheet. The particle mix in an Irish house is not the continental one the marketing is written for, and combustion is the awkward fraction: smoke from open fires, ranges and stoves across older estates produces PM2.5 and PM1 particles well under a micron, plus gases that no fibre filter touches at all. A unit optimised purely for pollen will underperform badly on a still winter evening.

How do you read an air purifier's CADR and room-size claim?

Coverage claims on the box — "cleans 90 m²" — are usually calculated at one air change per hour on maximum fan speed. One air change is close to useless; three to five is the target. Work from the CADR figure in cubic metres per hour instead and divide it by your room volume. A 250 m³/h unit in a 20 m² sitting room with 2.4 m ceilings, so 48 m³, delivers about five changes an hour, which is genuinely good. The same unit in a 45 m² open-plan space of 108 m³ manages barely two. Check which speed the CADR was measured at, too, because top speed on most machines is unusable in an occupied room. If a listing quotes only square metres and never gives a m³/h figure, the omission is the answer.

What does an air purifier really cost over five years?

Price the machine over five years, not at the checkout. Take a €200 mid-range unit with a €45 combined HEPA-and-carbon filter replaced annually: that is €225 in consumables. Electricity is larger than most people assume — 20 W running continuously is 175 kWh a year, which at Irish standard rates of roughly 38 cent a unit comes to about €67 a year, or €333 across five years. Running total: €758. Now take a €110 budget unit whose only filter costs €55 and is rated for six months: €550 of filters over the same period, usually with a less efficient motor as well. The cheaper box wins at the till and loses everywhere afterwards. Check the replacement filter's current price and Irish stock before buying the machine; that one search prevents most of the regret.

Which air purifier certifications actually mean something?

Three marks are worth looking for here. AHAM Verifide means the CADR figures were measured to a common test standard rather than self-declared. The ECARF seal and the Allergy UK Seal of Approval indicate that someone other than the manufacturer assessed allergen reduction — imperfect, but better than a brand's own bar chart. EN 1822 or ISO 29463 classification, the source of the H13 and H14 grades, tells you the media itself was tested, though it says nothing about whether the housing leaks around it. Gasketed, sealed filter chambers matter more than the grade on the sticker: air takes the easiest path, and on cheaply moulded bodies that path runs around the filter rather than through it. Absent all of these, ask whether the brand publishes a m³/h CADR and a dB(A) figure at a stated distance.

Buying in Ireland: filters, warranty and support

Physical retail for purifiers is thin here, so most purchases happen online through Amazon.ie, Currys, Harvey Norman, DID or a brand's own store. Two practical consequences follow. First, confirm the replacement filter has an Irish or EU fulfilment route; owner reports consistently mention long waits and unexpected customs charges on filters shipped from outside the EU, which quietly doubles their cost. Second, check the warranty is honoured by an EU entity, because posting a six-kilo appliance to a UK service address is rarely worth the trouble. Third-party filters exist for popular models at roughly half price, but some machines carry an RFID chip that refuses to reset the filter counter for a non-genuine part. That is not a dealbreaker, just a cost worth pricing in before the box is sitting in your hall.

Frequently asked questions

Which room should I put my first air purifier in?

The bedroom, in almost every case. You spend seven to nine hours a night in there with the door closed, which is both the longest continuous exposure of your day and by far the easiest volume for a purifier to actually clean — a sealed 12 m² room is a fair fight, an open-plan downstairs is not. A sitting room is the second choice if you have an open fire or a stove, or if someone in the house is home all day. Only add a second unit once the first is running continuously and you have judged whether it made a difference.

Do I need to run an air purifier all year in Ireland, or just in pollen season?

All year suits most Irish homes, because the triggers rotate rather than stop. Tree and grass pollen run from February through July, weed pollen and outdoor mould spores carry into September, and the damp months that follow bring indoor spores and solid-fuel smoke drifting from neighbouring chimneys. There is no genuinely clean month. Running continuously on a low or auto setting costs a few euro a month and stops particle levels rebounding, which they do within a couple of hours of the unit being switched off. Seasonal-only use makes sense if you have a single confirmed trigger and nothing else.

Can an air purifier replace opening the windows?

No, and they solve different problems. A purifier recirculates and filters air already in the room; it removes particles but does nothing about carbon dioxide, cooking moisture or the stale feel of an unventilated space. Irish homes, especially well-sealed newer builds, need ventilation to control humidity — and humidity control is what actually prevents mould forming in the first place. The workable compromise during pollen season is to ventilate briefly in the middle of the day or just after rain, when grass counts are lower, then close up and let the purifier deal with what came in.

Do air purifiers remove radon or carbon monoxide?

No. Radon is a radioactive gas and a real concern in parts of Ireland — the EPA publishes high-radon maps covering large areas of the west, midlands and southeast — but it is a gas, and HEPA media only captures particles. Activated carbon does not hold it either. Radon is dealt with through testing, sub-floor sumps and ventilation. Carbon monoxide is the same story: no domestic purifier removes it, and none is any substitute for a working CO alarm. If a listing claims otherwise, that alone tells you what the brand's other claims are worth.

How long does an air purifier take to clean a room?

Roughly the time needed for three to four air changes, so with a correctly sized unit you are looking at 30 to 60 minutes from a standing start on a medium speed. The arithmetic is simple: divide the CADR in m³/h by the room volume to get changes per hour. A 200 m³/h machine in a 48 m³ sitting room manages about four an hour, so most of the improvement lands inside the first twenty minutes. The catch is that particles keep arriving from outside and from ordinary activity, which is why continuous low-speed running beats occasional blasts on turbo.

Are third-party replacement filters worth buying?

Often, with two caveats. Generic filters for popular models typically cost 40–60% less, and the better ones use genuine H13 media, but there is no way to verify the grade from a listing photograph — buy from sellers with a long review history and check that the pleat count and depth match the original. The second issue is chipped filters: some manufacturers embed an RFID tag so the machine will not reset its counter for a non-genuine part. You can generally still run it, but the reminder light stays on. Factor that in when choosing the machine, not afterwards.

The ECARF seal and the Allergy UK Seal of Approval indicate that someone other than the manufacturer assessed allergen reduction — imperfect, but better than a brand's own bar chart. EN 1822 or ISO 29463 classification, the source of the H13 and H14 grades, tells you the media itself was tested, though it says nothing about whether the housing leaks around it. Gasketed, sealed filter chambers matter more than the grade on the sticker: air takes the easiest path, and on cheaply moulded bodies that path runs around the filter rather than through it. Absent all of these, ask whether the brand publishes a m³/h CADR and a dB(A) figure at a stated distance.

Buying in Ireland: filters, warranty and support

Physical retail for purifiers is thin here, so most purchases happen online through Amazon.ie, Currys, Harvey Norman, DID or a brand's own store. Two practical consequences follow. First, confirm the replacement filter has an Irish or EU fulfilment route; owner reports consistently mention long waits and unexpected customs charges on filters shipped from outside the EU, which quietly doubles their cost. Second, check the warranty is honoured by an EU entity, because posting a six-kilo appliance to a UK service address is rarely worth the trouble. Third-party filters exist for popular models at roughly half price, but some machines carry an RFID chip that refuses to reset the filter counter for a non-genuine part. That is not a dealbreaker, just a cost worth pricing in before the box is sitting in your hall.

Frequently asked questions

Which room should I put my first air purifier in?

The bedroom, in almost every case. You spend seven to nine hours a night in there with the door closed, which is both the longest continuous exposure of your day and by far the easiest volume for a purifier to actually clean — a sealed 12 m² room is a fair fight, an open-plan downstairs is not. A sitting room is the second choice if you have an open fire or a stove, or if someone in the house is home all day. Only add a second unit once the first is running continuously and you have judged whether it made a difference.

Do I need to run an air purifier all year in Ireland, or just in pollen season?

All year suits most Irish homes, because the triggers rotate rather than stop. Tree and grass pollen run from February through July, weed pollen and outdoor mould spores carry into September, and the damp months that follow bring indoor spores and solid-fuel smoke drifting from neighbouring chimneys. There is no genuinely clean month. Running continuously on a low or auto setting costs a few euro a month and stops particle levels rebounding, which they do within a couple of hours of the unit being switched off. Seasonal-only use makes sense if you have a single confirmed trigger and nothing else.

Can an air purifier replace opening the windows?

No, and they solve different problems. A purifier recirculates and filters air already in the room; it removes particles but does nothing about carbon dioxide, cooking moisture or the stale feel of an unventilated space. Irish homes, especially well-sealed newer builds, need ventilation to control humidity — and humidity control is what actually prevents mould forming in the first place. The workable compromise during pollen season is to ventilate briefly in the middle of the day or just after rain, when grass counts are lower, then close up and let the purifier deal with what came in.

Do air purifiers remove radon or carbon monoxide?

No. Radon is a radioactive gas and a real concern in parts of Ireland — the EPA publishes high-radon maps covering large areas of the west, midlands and southeast — but it is a gas, and HEPA media only captures particles. Activated carbon does not hold it either. Radon is dealt with through testing, sub-floor sumps and ventilation. Carbon monoxide is the same story: no domestic purifier removes it, and none is any substitute for a working CO alarm. If a listing claims otherwise, that alone tells you what the brand's other claims are worth.

How long does an air purifier take to clean a room?

Roughly the time needed for three to four air changes, so with a correctly sized unit you are looking at 30 to 60 minutes from a standing start on a medium speed. The arithmetic is simple: divide the CADR in m³/h by the room volume to get changes per hour. A 200 m³/h machine in a 48 m³ sitting room manages about four an hour, so most of the improvement lands inside the first twenty minutes. The catch is that particles keep arriving from outside and from ordinary activity, which is why continuous low-speed running beats occasional blasts on turbo.

Are third-party replacement filters worth buying?

Often, with two caveats. Generic filters for popular models typically cost 40–60% less, and the better ones use genuine H13 media, but there is no way to verify the grade from a listing photograph — buy from sellers with a long review history and check that the pleat count and depth match the original. The second issue is chipped filters: some manufacturers embed an RFID tag so the machine will not reset its counter for a non-genuine part. You can generally still run it, but the reminder light stays on. Factor that in when choosing the machine, not afterwards.
