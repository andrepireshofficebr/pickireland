Buying guide: how to choose

Why does speed matter more than efficiency for a bathroom heater?

A typical Irish bathroom is small enough in volume that even a modest-wattage heater can raise its temperature quickly — a 2m x 2m bathroom with a 2.4m ceiling is under 10 cubic metres, a fraction of a bedroom or sitting room. The catch is that bathroom heating isn't judged by whether a heater can eventually get the room warm, but by whether it gets there before you're out of the shower and cold. That makes speed of response matter more here than in any other room in the house. A slow-building oil radiator that would be ideal for an evening in the sitting room is the wrong tool here, however small the room is, because you need meaningful warmth within a minute or two, not fifteen to twenty. Radiant elements and fan heaters with PTC ceramic cores lead in this room specifically because they respond fast, not because they're more efficient.

IP ratings and bathroom zones explained

Irish and UK wiring regulations divide a bathroom into zones based on distance from water, and what electrical equipment is permitted in each zone is genuinely restricted, not just a manufacturer suggestion. IP21 is the minimum rating that qualifies a heater as splash-resistant enough for general bathroom use outside the immediate wet zones; IP24 offers stronger protection against splashing water from any direction and is the safer choice if the heater will sit anywhere near the shower or bath. A heater with no IP rating at all is a standard indoor appliance and has no business in a bathroom regardless of how far from the water it's positioned, because bathroom air itself carries far more humidity than a normal room even without direct splashing. Sockets are also restricted by zone, which is part of why many bathroom heaters are designed for pull-cord operation rather than a standard plug.

Recovery time: what 'ninety seconds to warm' really means

Manufacturer figures quoting a rapid warm-up time typically mean the point at which the heater itself reaches full output and starts putting out meaningful heat, not the point at which the room feels warm — those are two different things, and a small, poorly insulated bathroom with a cold tiled floor and no door draught seal can take noticeably longer to feel comfortable even with a heater that reaches full power almost instantly. Radiant and PTC fan heaters win on this measure because there's no thermal mass to bring up to temperature first, unlike an oil-filled radiator's oil reservoir. The practical takeaway is to treat quoted warm-up times as how fast the heater starts working, and expect the room itself to lag a little behind that, more so in an older house with a single-glazed bathroom window or an uninsulated exterior wall.

Running cost for short bursts

Bathroom heaters are typically used in short, sharp bursts rather than running for hours, which changes the maths compared with a sitting room heater. A 2kW heater run for fifteen minutes before and during a shower costs roughly €0.19 at the standard day rate of around €0.38 per kWh — €0.19 x 30 days comes to about €5.70 a month for a daily shower warm-up, a genuinely modest sum even though the wattage itself looks high next to a panel heater's 750-1000W. This is the one heater category in the house where reaching for the higher-wattage, faster-response option rarely costs much extra, because total runtime stays low regardless of how it's used. The maths changes if the heater gets left running well beyond the shower itself, which is where a built-in timer earns its keep.

Wall-mounted vs portable — what's actually allowed

A wall-mounted, hardwired or pull-cord bathroom heater installed by a qualified electrician in line with zone regulations is the more permanent and, in most cases, the safer solution, because it's fixed out of reach of water and doesn't rely on a trailing cable or a socket positioned somewhere it technically shouldn't be. A portable plug-in heater is only appropriate in a bathroom if it carries a genuine IP rating and the room's socket is positioned in a zone where it's actually permitted to be used — check this rather than assuming, since not every bathroom socket is rated for appliance use. For a rented property where a hardwired installation isn't an option, a properly IP-rated portable heater kept well clear of the bath, shower and any splash zone is the realistic compromise, not a substitute for a compliant fixed installation where one is possible.

Frequently asked questions

What IP rating do I need for a bathroom heater?

IP21 is the practical minimum for a heater used anywhere in a bathroom, since it provides basic protection against dripping water and light splashing. IP24 is the safer choice if the heater will be positioned anywhere near the shower or bath, since it protects against splashing from any direction rather than just from above. A heater with no IP rating at all is a standard indoor appliance and isn't designed for bathroom humidity, let alone splashing, regardless of how far it sits from the water source — bathroom air carries enough moisture on its own to be a problem for unrated electronics over time.

Can I plug a heater into a bathroom socket?

Only if both the socket and the heater are positioned and rated appropriately under Irish wiring regulations, which divide bathrooms into zones based on distance from water and restrict what can go in each one. Not every bathroom has a socket at all, and where one exists it's usually positioned specifically to sit outside the restricted zones. Rather than assuming a socket is fine to use, check its position relative to the bath or shower and make sure the heater itself carries an appropriate IP rating — the two need to line up together, not just one or the other.

How quickly should a bathroom heater actually warm the room?

A radiant or PTC ceramic fan heater typically reaches full output within seconds to under a minute, but that's the heater working, not the room feeling warm — a small tiled bathroom with a cold floor and no draught-proofing around the door can still take a few minutes to feel genuinely comfortable even once the heater is at full power. Manufacturer figures describe the heater's own warm-up, so treat quoted times as a starting point rather than a guarantee of how the room itself will feel, particularly in an older house with a single-glazed window or an uninsulated exterior wall in that room.

Is a wall-mounted heater better than a portable one for a bathroom?

In most cases, yes, primarily on safety grounds rather than performance. A fixed, properly zoned wall-mounted or pull-cord heater installed by a qualified electrician is kept clear of water by design and doesn't depend on a trailing cable, a portable unit's own footprint, or a socket that may or may not be correctly positioned for bathroom use. A portable heater can still be a reasonable choice, particularly in a rented property, provided it carries a genuine IP rating and is kept well clear of the bath, shower and any splash zone — but a compliant fixed installation is the more permanent and lower-risk option where it's available.

How much does it cost to run a heater for a 15-minute shower warm-up?

A 2kW heater run for fifteen minutes costs roughly €0.19 at the standard day rate of around €0.38 per kWh — the arithmetic is a quarter of the €0.76 hourly cost. Used daily for a month, that comes to about €5.70, a modest figure given how high the wattage looks on paper, because total runtime stays so low. This is one case where choosing a higher-wattage, faster-response heater over a lower-wattage slow one rarely costs much extra in practice, since the short burst pattern keeps the total electricity used low regardless of the heater's rated output.

Can I use a normal fan heater in the bathroom if I keep it away from the shower?

Distance alone doesn't make an unrated heater safe to use in a bathroom, because the humidity in the air itself, not just direct splashing, is enough to affect electronics that aren't designed for it over time, and Irish wiring regulations govern where electrical appliances can be used in a bathroom regardless of how far from the water they're positioned. A standard fan heater with no IP rating is designed for a dry room and should stay in one. If you want fan-heater style speed in a bathroom, look specifically for a model marketed and IP-rated for bathroom use rather than assuming a normal one is fine as long as it's a few feet from the water.

How much does it cost to run a heater for a 15-minute shower warm-up?

A 2kW heater run for fifteen minutes costs roughly €0.19 at the standard day rate of around €0.38 per kWh — the arithmetic is a quarter of the €0.76 hourly cost. Used daily for a month, that comes to about €5.70, a modest figure given how high the wattage looks on paper, because total runtime stays so low. This is one case where choosing a higher-wattage, faster-response heater over a lower-wattage slow one rarely costs much extra in practice, since the short burst pattern keeps the total electricity used low regardless of the heater's rated output.

Can I use a normal fan heater in the bathroom if I keep it away from the shower?

Distance alone doesn't make an unrated heater safe to use in a bathroom, because the humidity in the air itself, not just direct splashing, is enough to affect electronics that aren't designed for it over time, and Irish wiring regulations govern where electrical appliances can be used in a bathroom regardless of how far from the water they're positioned. A standard fan heater with no IP rating is designed for a dry room and should stay in one. If you want fan-heater style speed in a bathroom, look specifically for a model marketed and IP-rated for bathroom use rather than assuming a normal one is fine as long as it's a few feet from the water.
