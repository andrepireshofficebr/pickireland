Buying guide: how to choose

What kind of room does a panel heater actually work best in?

Panel heaters carry little thermal mass and rely on either convection or a flat infrared element to warm a room steadily rather than in a fast burst, which suits newer builds with a BER rating of B or A, tight window and door seals, and good attic and wall insulation. In that kind of airtight room the heat has nowhere to escape, so a modest wattage running in the background holds a comfortable temperature all evening. A panel heater's slim profile and wall-mounted design make it the closest thing to a fitted heating solution in the plug-in category, but that design is also what defines where it performs. Put the same heater in a draughty older room with single glazing and gaps around the frames and it will cycle almost constantly, trying to keep pace with heat leaking out as fast as it's produced — at which point wattage stops being the limiting factor and insulation becomes the real problem.

Smart scheduling: shifting use, not cost per watt

App-connected panel heaters let you schedule heating around your actual routine, or switch a heater off remotely if you've left it running by mistake, and that's a genuine convenience — but it's worth being clear about what it does and doesn't change. Every kWh still costs the same whether you switch the heater on with an app, a physical dial or a wall timer; a smart panel heater draws no less electricity per hour than a dumb one at the same wattage and thermostat setting. The saving comes entirely from not heating a room nobody's in, whether that's catching a heater left on after you've left for work or geofencing it to start warming up as you head home. If you're already disciplined about switching heaters off, app control adds convenience rather than a new source of savings.

Why panel heaters struggle in older or draughty homes

The same low thermal mass that makes panel heaters responsive and slim also means they have almost nothing in reserve once the element switches off — unlike an oil radiator, there's no stored warmth carrying the room through a thermostat's off cycle. In a well-sealed room that barely matters, because heat loss is slow enough that the panel doesn't need to fire often. In an older Irish home with a lower BER rating, solid walls, and draughts around original windows and doors, that same panel heater ends up cycling on almost as often as it cycles off, chasing heat that's escaping nearly as fast as it's produced. That's not a flaw in the heater — it's a mismatch between a background-heating tool and a room that needs bursts of stronger, more responsive heat, which an oil radiator or fan heater with proper thermal output is usually better suited to.

The maths on background heating

A 750W panel heater cycling at around 50% duty in a well-sealed modern bedroom, held at a steady background temperature over a six-hour evening, costs in the region of €1.43 at the standard day rate of roughly €0.38 per kWh. Move the same heater into a draughtier room where it ends up cycling closer to 85% of the time just to hold the same temperature, and the cost for the same six hours rises to roughly €2.42 — a substantial jump without changing the heater at all, purely down to how well the room holds what it's given. This is the clearest illustration of why panel heaters are sold on room suitability as much as wattage: the number on the box tells you what it can produce, not what it will actually cost you in a specific room.

Installation: wall-mounted vs portable, and safety clearances

Most panel heaters can either be fixed permanently to a wall bracket or left standing on integrated feet, and the choice affects both safety and how well the heater performs. Wall mounting keeps the heater clear of curtains, bedding and furniture, which matters because manufacturer guidance typically calls for a clearance of at least 50cm from anything flammable, more for a heater mounted below a window with curtains nearby. It also lets warm air or radiant heat spread across the room properly rather than being partly blocked by whatever the heater happens to be leaning against on the floor. If you want to use a panel heater in a bathroom, check the IP rating specifically — most standard panel heaters carry no water protection at all and are not rated for a bathroom, even mounted well away from the shower.

Frequently asked questions

Do panel heaters work in older Irish houses?

They'll work, but often less efficiently than in a newer, better-sealed home, because panel heaters have very little thermal mass and rely on the room holding heat well between cycles. In an older house with solid walls, single glazing or draughts around original windows and doors, a panel heater ends up cycling almost constantly to keep pace with heat escaping, which drives up runtime and cost compared with the same heater in a tightly sealed modern room. They're not a bad choice for an older home outright, but an oil radiator's thermal mass or a higher-wattage convector often copes better with that kind of heat loss.

What's the difference between infrared and convector panel heaters?

A convector panel heats the air passing over its element, which then circulates around the room, whereas an infrared panel radiates heat directly to solid objects and people in its path, similarly to how sunlight warms you even on a cold day. Infrared responds faster because it doesn't wait for air to circulate, and it can feel warmer at a given wattage if you're sitting directly in its path, but it does less to raise the overall air temperature of a room. Convector panels are the more common choice for background whole-room warmth; infrared suits a fixed spot like a desk or an armchair.

Can I leave a panel heater running in the background all day?

You can, and it's the use case panel heaters are actually designed around, but the cost adds up over a full day regardless of how low the wattage is. A 750W panel cycling at moderate duty in a well-sealed room might cost somewhere around €4-€5 for a twelve-hour day at the standard day rate — manageable for one occupied room, but worth comparing against simply heating the room for the hours you're actually in it rather than continuously. A basic timer or the heater's own scheduling function is usually a better balance than running it unattended from morning to night.

Do smart or WiFi panel heaters actually save money?

The electricity itself costs the same per kWh whether the heater is controlled by an app, a physical timer or a simple dial — smart features don't make the heating element more efficient. The saving comes from better control: not leaving a heater running in an empty room, scheduling it to start shortly before you're home rather than hours in advance, or switching it off remotely if you forget. If you're already disciplined about turning heaters off, a smart model mainly adds convenience rather than a new source of savings; if you regularly forget to switch heaters off, the app control can pay for the price difference fairly quickly.

Can panel heaters be installed in a bathroom?

Only specific models rated for it. Most standard panel heaters have no IP water-resistance rating at all and are not designed or approved for bathroom use, regardless of how far they're positioned from the shower or bath. If you want panel-style heating in a bathroom, look specifically for a model marketed as bathroom-safe with an IP21 or ideally IP24 rating, and be aware that Irish wiring regulations restrict where electrical appliances and sockets can be positioned relative to water sources — a hardwired, properly zoned installation is usually the safer route for a bathroom rather than a plug-in panel heater.

How much wall clearance does a panel heater need?

Manufacturer guidance commonly calls for a minimum of 50cm clearance from curtains, bedding, furniture or anything else flammable, and more if the heater sits below a window where curtains could brush against it when moved. This isn't a minor formality — panel heaters run hot enough on their front surface to pose a genuine fire risk if fabric is left resting against them for an extended period. Wall-mounting the heater at the recommended height, rather than propping a portable version against furniture, is the more reliable way to guarantee that clearance is maintained day to day.
