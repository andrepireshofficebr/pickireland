Buying guide: how to choose

How do you actually pick the right electric heater for a room?

Answer three questions before comparing wattage or price: how big is the room, how well does it hold heat, and how long will the heater run each time you switch it on. A draughty period-house bedroom with single glazing loses heat far faster than a sealed 2020s apartment with the same floor area, so the same heater behaves completely differently in each. Rooms you occupy for hours — a sitting room in the evening, a home office all day — suit heaters with thermal mass or accurate thermostats that hold a set temperature efficiently. Rooms you pass through briefly — a hallway, a bathroom before a shower — suit fast radiant or fan heat that warms a person, not the air. Getting this match wrong is the single biggest reason Irish households end up with a heater that either never feels warm enough or runs constantly and produces a bill to match.

Which electric heater features actually matter?

Ignore wattage badges and colour options and check four things instead. A proper thermostat — ideally digital, not a vague dial — cycles the element off once the room reaches temperature, which is where most of the real-world saving comes from. A timer or scheduling function lets you avoid heating an empty room before you get home. Tip-over and overheat cut-offs are non-negotiable if the heater will ever run unattended, and matter more in homes with pets, small children or timber floors. Lastly, check the IP rating if the heater might ever be used somewhere damp — a standard heater has no splash protection at all. Everything else, from remote controls to app connectivity, is convenience layered on top of these basics rather than a reason to choose one model over another.

What a 2kW heater really costs, hour by hour

Every resistive electric heater — oil-filled, panel, ceramic, fan — turns essentially 100% of the electricity it draws into heat, so cost is simple arithmetic: kilowatts × hours × unit price. At the current Electric Ireland standard day rate of roughly €0.38 per kWh, a 2kW heater run flat out for one hour costs about €0.76. Run it for a typical evening — say four hours while a thermostat cycles it on and off around 70% of the time — and you are looking at roughly €2.13. Leave it running for a full eight-hour overnight stretch at that duty cycle and the figure climbs past €4. None of this changes with the type of heater; a 2kW oil radiator and a 2kW fan heater cost the same to run at the same wattage. What differs is how much of that hour is spent actually drawing power, which is where thermostat quality and room insulation do the real work.

Is it cheaper to heat one room electrically than run central heating?

Firing up a gas or oil boiler to heat an entire house just to warm the one room you're sitting in is usually the more expensive option, especially in older homes with a lower BER rating where a lot of that heat is lost before it reaches you. A single 2kW electric heater running for an evening in one room can genuinely beat the cost of a whole central heating cycle. The comparison flips the other way if you try to heat an entire house electrically: gas and oil remain cheaper per kWh delivered than electric resistive heat once you're warming every room rather than one. The practical rule for most Irish homes is to let central heating handle the whole house at a lower background temperature and use a plug-in electric heater to top up the one room you're actually using, rather than choosing one system exclusively.

Where should you buy an electric heater in Ireland?

Most electric heaters sold here come from Woodies, Harvey Norman, Currys, Argos, Litely-style discounters or Amazon.ie, and prices for a given model are usually within a few euro across all of them, so it's worth a quick check before buying rather than assuming any one retailer is cheapest. UK-market listings occasionally slip through on Amazon with a UK plug rather than an Irish one — check the plug type in the images, not just the listing text. A CE mark is the baseline for anything sold legally in Ireland; it is not a performance claim. Panel heaters over a certain wattage carry an EU energy label, which is useful for comparing thermostat sophistication between models but says nothing about how a heater suits your specific room. Standard consumer protection gives you a two-year warranty regardless of what a listing advertises separately.

Frequently asked questions

Which type of electric heater is cheapest to run overall?

There's no single winner, because running cost depends on wattage and how long the thermostat keeps the element on, not the heater type. A low-wattage panel or radiant heater aimed at a person for short bursts will usually cost less over a week than a high-wattage convector left cycling all evening, simply because it draws fewer watts for less time. Two heaters of the same wattage cost exactly the same per hour of actual running, whatever the marketing says. The practical answer is to buy the lowest wattage that still makes you comfortable for how you actually use the room, and pair it with an accurate thermostat so it isn't running longer than it needs to.

How much does a 2kW heater cost per hour in Ireland?

At the current standard day rate of around €0.38 per kWh, a 2kW heater running continuously for one hour costs about €0.76. In practice a thermostatically controlled heater rarely runs the full hour once the room is up to temperature — if it's cycling at roughly 60-70% duty, the real hourly cost drops to somewhere between €0.46 and €0.53. On a cheaper night-rate tariff, where rates can fall to €0.15-€0.22 per kWh depending on supplier, the same hour costs €0.30-€0.44, though most evening heater use falls in the day-rate window rather than the overnight one.

Is it safe to leave any electric heater on overnight?

Only if the model has genuine overheat and tip-over protection, and even then most manufacturers and fire safety guidance recommend against leaving any heater running unattended in a room with no one awake to notice a fault. Oil-filled radiators with a sealed element and a proper thermostat are generally considered the safer option for long unattended stretches because there's no exposed heating coil, but 'safer' isn't the same as 'recommended for every night.' If overnight warmth is the goal, a timer that switches the heater off an hour or two after you fall asleep is a more sensible approach than leaving it running until morning.

What size heater do I need for a home office?

For a typical Irish box-room office of 8-10 m² with reasonable insulation, 1000-1500W is usually enough to hold a comfortable working temperature once the room is up to heat, especially if the door stays closed. A poorly insulated period-house room of the same size, or one with a large single-glazed window, often needs the full 2000W to keep pace with heat loss. Because you're sitting still for hours rather than moving around, a heater aimed at you directly — a panel or radiant model near the desk — often feels warmer at a lower wattage than a convector trying to heat the whole room's air.

Do I need a smart plug or timer if the heater doesn't have one built in?

It helps, but it isn't a substitute for a proper built-in thermostat. A smart plug can switch a heater off at a set time or by schedule, which stops you leaving it running in an empty room, but it can't respond to the room actually reaching temperature the way a thermostat does — it just turns the whole heater on or off on a timer, at full output, regardless of how warm the room already is. For real savings, a heater with its own accurate thermostat is doing more useful work than a dumb heater on a smart plug, though combining both gives you the most control.
