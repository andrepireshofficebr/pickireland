Buying guide: how to choose

How do you actually pick the right e-bike category for your commute?

Work out which category you need before comparing wattage and battery size. A flat 4km commute on quiet roads barely needs assistance, so a mid-range hub-motor bike is plenty; add hills, headwind or 10km-plus each way and torque starts to matter more than top speed; apartment living or a shared hallway points toward a folding frame; and weekend trail riding needs wider tyres, more suspension travel and a frame rated for repeated impact. The five bikes below span four very different use cases, and the best one depends entirely on yours. Skipping this step is why so many Irish e-bike buyers end up swapping bikes within a year: the spec sheet was good, the category was wrong.

The €1,500 Cycle to Work ceiling and where it bites

Cycle to Work covers up to €1,500 for an e-bike and safety equipment, versus €1,250 for a standard bicycle and €3,000 for a cargo bike, and it can be used once every four tax years. The saving comes from salary sacrifice: the amount comes off your gross pay before income tax, USC and PRSI, so a higher-rate taxpayer paying roughly 52% in combined deductions saves close to that proportion of the bike's price. On a bike priced exactly at €1,500, that is a real-world saving of several hundred euro spread across the repayment period, typically 12 months. The catch is the ceiling itself: anything priced above €1,500 still qualifies, but only the first €1,500 gets the tax relief, and the balance is paid normally. Check with your employer's payroll provider before ordering, because not every employer has the scheme set up, and some cap the annual amount lower than Revenue allows.

Legal status: pedelec vs moped, and why it matters when buying

Every bike here needs to be a pedelec to count as a bicycle under Irish law: pedal-assist only, motor output capped at 250W continuous, and assistance that cuts out at 25km/h. Meet those limits and you need no licence, no insurance and no registration — it's legally a bicycle, full stop. Cross into throttle-controlled territory, where the motor moves the bike without pedalling, and Irish law reclassifies it as a motor vehicle, which drags in registration, insurance and a licence most buyers don't have. Speed pedelecs that assist up to 45km/h fall into an L1e-B moped category with the same requirements. None of the bikes compared below are throttle-only or speed pedelecs, but it's worth checking this yourself on any bike you're considering outside this list — the spec sheet should say 250W and 25km/h explicitly, and if it doesn't, ask before buying.

Hub motor vs mid-drive, and why it shows up in every category here

Every bike in this comparison uses a hub motor rather than a mid-drive, and that's not an accident — hub motors are cheaper to build, simpler to service, and dominate the sub-€2,000 Irish market almost completely. A hub motor sits in the wheel and pushes directly, which works fine on flat and rolling terrain but loses efficiency on steep, sustained climbs because it can't use the bike's gears to multiply torque the way a mid-drive can. Mid-drives, mounted at the pedals, feel more natural and climb better, but they cost considerably more and concentrate wear on the drivetrain. For commuting on Irish roads with the occasional hill, a hub motor is a sensible trade-off. For repeated steep climbing — the kind found on eMTB routes — the torque disadvantage becomes more noticeable, which is worth remembering if your riding leans toward trails rather than tarmac.

What breaks first, and where to get it fixed in Ireland

Across owner reports, the parts that fail first are predictable: budget tyres puncture more on Irish roads' broken glass and hedge-cutting debris, cheap cable-actuated brakes lose bite in the wet faster than hydraulic ones, and connectors at the battery-to-controller junction corrode if they're not sealed well after a winter of rain. None of that is specific to one brand — it's a function of price point. Before buying, check whether the seller has an Irish or EU-based service point, because posting a battery or motor back to a warehouse outside the EU for warranty work routinely takes months and can trigger customs charges on the way back. Independent bike shops around Ireland increasingly service e-bikes regardless of brand, but they need the battery's specific charging profile and sometimes proprietary tools for motor diagnostics, so ask before assuming any shop can fix any bike.

Frequently asked questions

What's the real difference between a hub-motor and a mid-drive e-bike?

Hub motors sit in the wheel and push directly; mid-drives sit at the pedals and drive through the bike's own gears. That gearing means a mid-drive delivers more usable torque on steep climbs and feels more like pedalling a very fit version of yourself, while a hub motor feels more like being pushed from behind. Mid-drives cost more — typically several hundred euro more at the same spec level — and concentrate extra wear on the chain and cassette, which need replacing more often. For flat-to-rolling Irish commuting, hub motors are the sensible default and are what nearly every bike under €2,000 sold here uses. Mid-drives earn their price on eMTBs and long, hilly commutes.

Do all e-bikes here qualify for Cycle to Work?

Any of these bikes qualify as long as your employer has the Cycle to Work scheme set up and processes the purchase through payroll — the scheme applies to the bike, not the specific retailer, provided it's a genuine pedal-assist bicycle rather than a throttle-controlled vehicle. The relief covers up to €1,500 of an e-bike's price; anything above that is paid normally without tax relief. Confirm with your payroll or HR contact before ordering, since some employers require you to buy through a specific registered retailer or portal rather than any seller, and the paperwork needs to be in place before you pay, not after.

How long do e-bike batteries actually last before they need replacing?

Most lithium-ion e-bike batteries are rated for 500 to 800 full charge cycles before capacity drops to around 70-80% of new, which for a typical commuter charging every couple of days works out to roughly three to five years of useful life. Heat, deep discharges and leaving a battery fully charged in storage all accelerate that decline, while partial charges and storing at around 50% over winter slow it down. Replacement batteries for popular models generally cost a few hundred euro, so factor that into the bike's real cost rather than treating the purchase price as final. Ask about standalone battery availability and price before buying, not after the original dies.

Is it worth buying an e-bike directly online versus from a local shop?

Online-only purchases from Amazon-style marketplaces are usually cheaper and offer far more choice, and that's reflected in most of the models compared here. The trade-off is assembly, initial setup and warranty handling — a local shop will build the bike properly, check brake and gear adjustment, and give you somewhere to walk in if something's wrong. Buying online means doing that setup yourself or paying a shop separately to check it over, and warranty claims go through the retailer rather than a face you know. Neither approach is wrong, but budget an extra hour of setup time and possibly a shop visit if you buy online.

What does an e-bike actually cost to own over five years?

Purchase price is only part of it. Add a replacement battery around year three to four (a few hundred euro), tyres roughly every 12-18 months depending on mileage, brake pads twice a year for daily commuters, and an annual service if you don't do it yourself. Electricity is negligible — a full charge on a typical 400-500Wh battery costs around 15-20 cent at current rates. Realistically, budget an extra 20-30% of the purchase price spread across five years for consumables and one battery replacement. A Cycle to Work purchase reduces the upfront hit but doesn't touch these ongoing costs, so factor them in when comparing a cheap bike against a pricier one with better base components.

Can I convert a normal bike into an e-bike instead of buying new?

Yes, conversion kits are widely available and can be cheaper than a new bike if you already own a decent frame, but the legal limits are identical — the finished conversion still needs to cap out at 250W continuous and cut assistance at 25km/h to count as a bicycle rather than a motor vehicle. Quality varies hugely between kits, and a poorly fitted hub motor or battery mount can strain a frame that wasn't designed for the extra weight and torque. For most buyers, a purpose-built e-bike with an integrated frame, matched components and a proper warranty is the safer choice; conversion suits someone mechanically confident with a bike worth keeping.
