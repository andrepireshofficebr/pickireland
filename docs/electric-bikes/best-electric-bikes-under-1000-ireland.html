Buying guide: how to choose

What actually gets cut on an electric bike under €1,000?

At this price point the corners cut are consistent across brands: battery cells are less likely to come from a recognisable manufacturer like Samsung or LG, motor controllers are simpler with less overload protection, and brakes are almost always mechanical disc rather than hydraulic. None of that automatically makes a bike unsafe, but it does mean more variance in how a battery ages and less stopping power in the wet. Frame welds and paint finish are usually fine even on budget bikes, since frames are commodity manufacturing and cheap to get right. What's genuinely hard to see from a listing is the battery management system (BMS) — the circuit that stops cells overcharging or overheating — and that's exactly where budget corners get cut without ever showing up in the spec sheet. It only becomes obvious in how the battery behaves after a year of use.

How do you read an e-bike battery spec sheet?

Battery size is written as voltage times amp-hours, and multiplying the two gives you watt-hours (Wh), which is the number that actually predicts range — a 36V 10Ah battery is 360Wh, a 48V 10Ah battery is 480Wh, and bigger Wh means more range at the same efficiency. Manufacturer range claims are typically measured on flat ground, in eco mode, with a light rider and no wind, so treat any quoted figure as a ceiling rather than an estimate. For real Irish riding — hills, a headwind, a rider closer to 85kg than 65kg, using mid-assist rather than eco — expect 40 to 60% of the advertised number. A bike claiming 50km range should realistically be budgeted at 20-30km for a hillier commute, which matters enormously if you're deciding between two models with different claimed ranges.

What does a €999 e-bike actually cost through Cycle to Work?

A sub-€1,000 e-bike gets the full benefit of Cycle to Work with room to spare under the €1,500 ceiling, and the maths is straightforward through salary sacrifice. Take a €999 bike: a higher-rate taxpayer paying 40% income tax plus 4% PRSI and 8% USC on that slice of income saves roughly 52% through the scheme, bringing the net cost to around €480, typically repaid over 12 months from gross salary. A standard-rate taxpayer on 20% tax saves closer to 28-32% combined, landing nearer €680-720 net. Either way, the saving is proportionally larger relative to the bike's price on a cheaper bike than on one near the €1,500 ceiling, because the full purchase price sits inside the relief. Confirm your employer runs the scheme and processes it through payroll before ordering — the discount only applies if the paperwork is done correctly.

Warranty and parts: the budget-tier risk

Most sub-€1,000 e-bikes sold in Ireland come from brands selling primarily through Amazon or their own web store rather than through a network of local dealers, which changes how warranty claims work in practice. A faulty controller or display usually means shipping the part back and waiting for a replacement rather than walking into a shop, and turnaround varies from a week to over a month depending on where the brand's EU warehouse is based. Before buying, check whether the listing or brand site mentions an EU or Irish return address specifically, not just an EU delivery address — those are different things. Also check whether spare batteries and chargers are sold separately for that exact model; budget brands sometimes discontinue a battery type within a year or two, leaving working bikes with no replacement available.

When is a cheap e-bike a false economy?

The single biggest false economy at this price point is a battery that fails at year two rather than year four, because a replacement can cost 30-40% of what the whole bike cost new — on a €700 bike, that's a €250-300 hit that erases most of the original saving over a pricier model. The second is corrosion: budget e-bikes are more likely to use unsealed connectors and painted rather than anodised or stainless fasteners, both of which degrade faster under Ireland's frequent rain. Wiping the bike down after wet rides and keeping it under cover extends component life noticeably, but it's an extra habit a pricier bike with better sealing doesn't demand as strictly. None of this means avoid budget e-bikes — it means budget for a second-year check-up and treat the purchase price as the start of the cost, not the whole of it.

Frequently asked questions

Are sub-€1,000 e-bikes safe to ride daily?

Yes, provided the bike has disc brakes (mechanical is acceptable, hydraulic is better), a battery from a bike-specific brand rather than an unbranded pack, and CE or UKCA marking on the battery and charger. Safety at this price comes down to the electrical system more than the frame — a poorly made battery pack is the main real risk, not the mechanical build. Check reviews specifically for battery or charging complaints rather than general ride-quality gripes, since that's where budget bikes most often go wrong. Daily commuting use is exactly what these bikes are built for; the main difference from a pricier bike is how the components age over two to three years, not whether they're safe on day one.

How long will a budget e-bike battery last before it needs replacing?

Expect somewhat less than the 500-800 charge cycles typical of better bikes — budget packs often show noticeable capacity loss by 300-400 cycles, which for a daily commuter is roughly 18 months to two years rather than three to five. Storing the bike with a part-charged battery over winter rather than fully charged or fully flat helps considerably, as does avoiding fast-charging every single time if a standard charger option exists. When shopping, check whether the exact battery model is still sold as a spare before buying the bike — some budget brands discontinue battery packs within a couple of years, which can turn a working bike into scrap if the original pack fails.

Can I get spare parts for cheap e-bike brands in Ireland?

For the mechanical parts — brakes, tyres, cranks, cables — yes, almost always, since these are standard bicycle components sold at any bike shop regardless of the frame's brand. For electrical parts — battery, controller, display, motor — it depends entirely on whether the brand maintains EU stock and sells spares separately from complete bikes. Check the brand's own website or Amazon storefront for standalone battery and charger listings before buying; if none exist, that's a signal spares may be hard to source later. Independent e-bike specialists in larger Irish towns increasingly stock generic controllers and displays that work across several budget brands, a useful fallback if the original part becomes unavailable.

Is a mechanical disc brake good enough, or should I hold out for hydraulic?

Mechanical disc brakes are genuinely fine for most commuting use and are what the majority of sub-€1,000 e-bikes ship with — they need more frequent cable adjustment than hydraulic and require slightly more hand force for the same stopping power, but they stop the bike safely. The gap widens in Irish rain: hydraulic systems maintain consistent bite when wet, while mechanical systems can feel noticeably softer until the pads and rim clear water. If your commute is mostly dry, flat and short, mechanical is a non-issue. If it includes descents, heavy traffic braking, or you ride through winter regardless of weather, hydraulic brakes are worth paying extra for even within this budget bracket.

Do budget e-bikes hold their resale value?

Not well, generally. Unbranded or lesser-known e-bike brands depreciate faster than established names because buyers are wary of unknown battery history and harder-to-source spares on the second-hand market, so expect to recoup considerably less than half the purchase price after two years even in good condition. This matters less if you're planning to run the bike into the ground rather than upgrade, but it's worth factoring in if part of the appeal of a budget bike is trying e-biking before committing to a pricier model later — you likely won't recover much of the price difference by selling.

What's the cheapest way to legally get an e-bike in Ireland?

Cycle to Work is the cheapest legal route for anyone on PAYE with an employer that runs the scheme, since it applies income tax, USC and PRSI relief to the full price of a sub-€1,000 bike, typically cutting 28-52% off depending on your tax rate. Outside the scheme, buying during sales periods (January and pre-summer are typical for stock clearance) and choosing last season's model over the newest release both bring meaningful savings. Avoid grey-market imports advertised well below typical pricing for a given spec — they often lack CE marking, EU warranty support, or comply with different power limits than Irish law allows, which can cause problems later even if the upfront price looks attractive.
