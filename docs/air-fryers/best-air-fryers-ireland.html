Buying guide: how to choose

What actually makes an air fryer's air circulation work?

A real air fryer mounts a fan above a perforated basket, forcing hot air down and around the food rather than just heating a sealed drawer like a mini oven. Cheaper models fuse the element and fan into a single housing over a shallow basket, which browns the top layer well but leaves anything underneath steaming in its own moisture. Every countertop box now calls itself an air fryer, so the giveaway is basket depth relative to width: a wide, shallow basket spreads a single layer of chips or nuggets properly, while a deep, narrow one forces stacking, and stacking is what makes shaking halfway through non-negotiable rather than optional. Manufacturer figures rarely mention shake frequency, so check the basket photos before the spec sheet — a tall, letterbox-shaped drawer usually means interrupting the cook twice, not once.

Capacity numbers that don't match what fits

The litre figure on the box measures the basket's total volume, not the usable cooking space once you allow for airflow clearance around the food. A drawer rated at 4.2 litres realistically holds two adult portions of chips plus a protein item spread in a loose single layer, not stacked. For the typical Irish household, that means a couple is comfortable at 2-3.5 litres, a family of four wants 5.5-7.6 litres or a dual-zone split, and anyone regularly feeding five or more should look at 8 litres and up or accept cooking in two rounds. The honest test before buying is picturing a full tray of frozen chips, not a handful, sitting in a single layer inside that basket. If the listing photos only ever show the basket half full, that's usually because a genuinely full load doesn't circulate properly.

The real energy sum: air fryer versus oven

Run the numbers instead of trusting a marketing claim of 'up to 70% less energy.' An electric oven drawing roughly 2.5kW needs about 10 minutes to preheat plus a further 40 minutes cooking a typical tray bake — 50 minutes total, or 0.83 hours. At 2.5kW that's 2.08 kWh, and at the current average Irish unit rate of around 38 cent per kWh, that single meal costs roughly 79 cent in electricity alone. An air fryer at 1.6kW needs no preheat and typically finishes the same food in 20-25 minutes — call it 22.5 minutes, or 0.375 hours — for 0.6 kWh and about 23 cent. That's a saving near 56 cent every time you cook. Do that for five evening meals a week and the air fryer saves roughly €14.50 a month. Evening cooking sits inside peak hours on every tariff, so a smart meter's cheaper night rate doesn't change this particular comparison.

What actually breaks first

Failure points on most machines cluster in three places: the non-stick coating on the basket, the drawer's slide mechanism, and the digital display. Coating wear shows up first on baskets used daily without a silicone liner or light oil spray, typically within 12-18 months of heavy use, and owner reports consistently mention recoating or replacing the basket as the reason a working machine eventually gets binned. The slide mechanism — plastic rails letting the drawer click in and lock — takes the most physical abuse from daily use and is the part most likely to loosen on budget models first. Under Irish and EU consumer law, appliances carry a minimum two-year manufacturer guarantee, and it's worth confirming the seller is EU-based before buying, since claims against UK-only retailers post-Brexit now involve customs friction that erodes any saving. A basket explicitly marked dishwasher-safe, rather than merely wipeable, is worth prioritising over almost any other spec.

Where the extra money actually buys something

Spend under €50 and you're generally buying a single-speed, dial-controlled basket with no shake reminder and a coating unlikely to survive two years of regular dishwasher cycles — fine for occasional use, frustrating for daily cooking. Between €60 and €110 is where digital controls, presets and a shake reminder become standard, and where build quality first turns genuinely reliable rather than a lucky draw. Above €110, the money increasingly buys dual-zone functionality, larger capacity or app connectivity rather than better core cooking, since crisping performance itself plateaus once the fan and basket geometry are competent. The exception is genuinely large households, where the jump to 8+ litres or a split basket solves a real batch-cooking problem rather than adding a feature nobody touches. Match the spend to the actual gap in your kitchen — capacity for families, reliability and simplicity for everyone else.

Frequently asked questions

How much does an air fryer actually cost to run per month in Ireland?

It depends on frequency, but a realistic middle case is five evening meals a week at roughly 0.6 kWh per cook and 38 cent per unit, working out to about 23 cent per meal, or €5-6 a month for a household cooking dinner this way five nights out of seven. Heavier use — daily cooking plus reheats and snacks — pushes that closer to €10-15 a month. Either figure sits well below running an oven for the same meals, where preheating alone typically adds 15-20 cent to every single use. The bigger monthly saving usually isn't the electricity itself, it's the meals that would otherwise have become a takeaway because the oven felt like too much effort for one portion.

Is a more expensive air fryer actually better, or just bigger?

Past roughly €110, extra spend mostly buys capacity, a second independent zone, or connected features, not meaningfully better crisping. Forced-air cooking plateaus once a machine has a competent fan and a properly shaped basket, and both arrive well before the top of the price range. Higher prices are genuinely justified for households that need the extra litres or two simultaneous zones for real batch cooking; paying for capacity you'll use every week is different from paying for a badge on the front. Check basket dimensions and stated wattage before the price tag — two machines at €70 and €180 can heat almost identically and differ mainly in how much food they hold.

Do I need a dual-zone air fryer, or is a single basket enough?

For one or two people, a single basket almost always covers it. Dual-zone earns its keep once you're regularly cooking a main and a side that finish at different times, which is mostly a three-plus person household problem. A single basket around 4.5-5.5 litres handles sequential cooking fine for smaller households — the second item goes in while the first rests, costing a few minutes rather than much at all. Buy dual-zone for the synchronised finish, not the extra capacity alone, since one large basket often holds close to as much food as two small zones combined once you account for the divider between them.

Can an air fryer fully replace my oven?

For most weeknight cooking, yes — chips, nuggets, chicken pieces, roasted vegetables, reheating, even a fair amount of baking in the larger multi-cooker style units. Where it can't compete is anything needing real volume or specific oven geometry: a full roasting tray for six, a large cake tin, or dishes that need steady ambient heat around the food rather than direct airflow, like a big lasagne. Most Irish households that make the switch end up keeping the oven for Sunday roasts and big-batch cooking, and leaning on the air fryer for the other six days of the week. It replaces daily use convincingly; it doesn't replace the appliance itself for every job.

How long do air fryers actually last before something goes wrong?

Two to four years of near-daily use is a realistic expectation before the basket coating degrades enough to need replacing or the unit itself develops a fault, based on the pattern across owner reviews at every price point. Machines under €50 tend to cluster at the low end of that range; models with metal internal components and a stated two-year warranty skew longer. The single biggest lifespan factor within your control is the basket coating — hand-washing with a soft sponge instead of abrasive pads or a harsh dishwasher cycle roughly doubles how long it stays properly non-stick before food starts catching on it.

What's the single feature most worth paying extra for?

A shake reminder paired with an accurately proportioned basket, not a screen or app control. Uneven cooking is the most common complaint in Irish reviews, and it's almost always a shaking problem rather than a fundamental fault — either the owner forgot, or the basket's proportions made a proper shake impossible without half the contents landing on the counter. A timer that pauses and prompts partway through the cook solves this directly, and it's available on machines well under €100. Everything layered on top of that price point is convenience rather than an improvement in how the food actually turns out.
