Buying guide: how to choose

Rotating pads versus vibrating pads versus static cloths

Three distinct mopping mechanisms are sold under the same mop-combo label, and they perform very differently. Static cloth pads, common at the lower end of this category, are dragged behind the unit and rely entirely on dampness and friction — fine for dust, ineffective on anything dried-on. Vibrating pads add rapid back-and-forth motion to loosen light marks without scrubbing pressure. Rotating pads, reserved for mid-range and premium models, spin under downward pressure the way a manual scrubbing motion would, and are the only mechanism of the three that manufacturer spec sheets consistently describe as capable of lifting actual stains rather than just wiping surface dust. None of the three is a substitute for a hands-and-knees scrub of a genuinely dirty floor, but the gap between static and rotating in daily maintenance mopping is large enough to be worth checking specifically rather than assuming mop function means the same thing across every listing.

Carpet in the house: lifting versus avoidance

Every mop combo needs a strategy for carpet, since a wet pad dragged across carpet either soaks it or, worse, redistributes dirty water into the fibres. The two approaches on the market are lifting — the mop mechanism physically raises a few millimetres off the floor when the robot detects carpet — and avoidance, where the robot's map routes it around carpeted rooms entirely during a mopping run. Lifting is the more useful mechanism for a typical Irish home with carpet and hard floor on the same level, since it allows one continuous run to vacuum everywhere and mop only the hard-floor sections without needing two separate scheduled cleans. Avoidance-only models require carpeted rooms to be manually excluded from the mop schedule via the app, which works but adds a setup step lifting doesn't need. Checking which mechanism a specific model uses is more useful than trusting a generic carpet-safe label.

Self-washing docks and what they actually save you

Self-washing docks rinse the mop pad in hot or cold water and dry it with warm air between cleans, which solves the least pleasant part of mop-combo ownership: manually removing and rinsing a dirty pad, and the mildew smell that develops if a damp pad is left on the robot or in a drawer. It's a genuine quality-of-life upgrade, not just a premium-tier gimmick, particularly in an Irish home where a damp pad left sitting for even a day or two in typical indoor humidity can start to smell. What it doesn't solve is the underlying water tank — someone still needs to refill clean water and empty dirty water periodically, generally every one to two weeks depending on how often the mop runs, so self-washing reduces manual mop-handling but doesn't eliminate all human involvement in the mopping cycle.

Detergent, hot water and the running cost of mopping

Mopping adds a running cost vacuuming alone doesn't have: cleaning solution or detergent, sold as an add-on by most brands offering self-washing docks, plus more frequent mop pad replacement than the vacuum-only brush and filter schedule. Manufacturer-recommended detergent isn't strictly required — plain water works for daily maintenance mopping — but brands market their own solution as improving grease-cutting on kitchen floors specifically, and using it adds a modest ongoing cost on top of the vacuum-only consumables already budgeted for elsewhere. Replacement mop pads themselves, sold in packs, are a relatively small addition to overall running costs but do wear out faster with heavier daily use, particularly on rotating-pad models where the pad experiences physical friction rather than just dampness. Factoring in an extra ongoing amount specifically for mopping consumables, on top of standard vacuum maintenance, gives a more honest running-cost picture than looking at vacuum-only figures alone.

Where mopping genuinely earns its keep in an Irish home

Mopping earns its keep fastest in Irish kitchens and hallways — tiled or sealed-wood entryways that take the brunt of eight-plus months of wet shoes a year, and kitchen floors exposed to daily cooking splashes and pet water-bowl spills. A hallway that catches rain-wet shoe prints daily benefits from a genuine daily light mop far more than a spare room that sees occasional foot traffic, which is worth factoring into which rooms actually get scheduled for mopping rather than mopping the whole floor plan by default. It earns its keep slowest on rooms with minimal traffic or on floor types unsuited to it — natural unsealed wood or certain stone floors can be damaged by regular damp mopping, so checking floor-type compatibility before scheduling mop runs there matters more than the mopping mechanism itself.

Frequently asked questions

Will a robot mop damage real wood floors?

It depends on the finish, not the wood itself — sealed or engineered wood with a proper lacquer or oil finish generally tolerates light, well-wrung robot mopping fine, since the moisture level used is deliberately low. Unsealed, oiled-only, or older worn wood floors are more vulnerable to water damage over repeated exposure, and manufacturer guidance for most brands explicitly recommends excluding these floor types from scheduled mopping via the app's room settings. If in doubt about a specific floor's finish, checking with the flooring manufacturer or installer is safer than assuming the robot's water level is automatically low enough to be harmless everywhere.

Does the mop pad need washing separately even with a self-washing dock?

Most models, most of the time, no — a working self-washing dock rinses the pad thoroughly enough for continuous reuse across many cleans without manual intervention. Owner reports do mention that grease or heavier kitchen residue can build up gradually even with automated rinsing, meaning an occasional manual hand-wash every few weeks keeps the pad performing at its best rather than slowly declining. This is a maintenance-quality issue rather than a fault — the automated rinse cycle is genuinely doing most of the work, but it's not infinitely self-sufficient on a heavily used kitchen floor.

Can a mop combo leave streaks on tile floors?

Occasionally, particularly with harder water, which is common in parts of Ireland with limestone-heavy groundwater, since mineral deposits in tap water can leave a faint film after repeated mopping with plain water. Using a small amount of manufacturer-approved detergent generally reduces this, as does periodically wiping the floor manually to catch any buildup the robot's pad redistributes rather than fully lifts. It's a cosmetic issue rather than a functional one, and considerably less noticeable on matte or textured tile than on high-gloss polished tile, where any residue shows more readily under light.

Is a vacuum-only robot plus a separate manual mop cheaper overall than a combo?

Often yes on upfront cost, since a decent vacuum-only lidar model plus an inexpensive manual spray mop can cost less than a mid-range combo unit, though it trades away the daily automated mopping the combo provides. Whether that trade is worth it depends on how often the floors actually need mopping — a household with mostly carpet and light hard-floor traffic gains little from an automated mop and is better served putting the budget difference toward a better vacuum-only unit instead. A household with heavy kitchen and hallway traffic gets more genuine daily value from the combo's automation than the price difference would suggest.

How much water does a robot mop use per clean?

Manufacturer figures vary by tank size and mopping mode, but a typical mid-size tank on a moderate mopping setting covers roughly 80-150 square metres before needing a refill, broadly enough for one full ground-floor run in an average Irish semi-detached or terraced house. Larger open-plan layouts or a setting using more water per pass may need a mid-clean refill, which most current models pause for and resume automatically once topped up via the dock's water tank rather than requiring the whole clean to be restarted from scratch.
