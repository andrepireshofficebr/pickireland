Buying guide: how to choose

Why can't a robot vacuum clean an upstairs and downstairs on its own?

Robot vacuums don't climb stairs, full stop, and that single fact shapes the purchase more than any spec on the box. The typical Irish semi-detached or terraced house puts bedrooms on the first floor and kitchen and sitting room below, and a single robot only ever covers the level it's parked on. Owners either carry the unit between floors manually, buy a second cheaper unit for upstairs, or accept that one floor gets robot coverage and the other gets a stick vacuum on Saturdays. None of this is a flaw in any particular model; it's true of every robot vacuum sold, flagship or budget. Work out which floor genuinely needs the automation — usually the one with the most foot traffic or the pet — before comparing navigation systems or suction figures, because the floor-coverage question decides more about satisfaction than which lidar sensor is inside.

What do you actually gain moving up a robot vacuum tier?

Moving from budget to mid-range mainly buys you lidar or vision-based mapping instead of random-bounce navigation, which means rooms get covered systematically instead of by repeated overlapping passes — faster cleans, better battery use, and no-go zones you can actually draw on a floor plan. Moving from mid-range to premium buys automation depth, not raw cleaning power: self-emptying bins that stretch manual intervention from days to weeks, mop pads that wash and dry themselves instead of smearing dirty water around, and obstacle cameras that recognise cables and pet mess rather than just bumping into them. Suction figures published by manufacturers climb across all three tiers but matter far less in practice than brush design and floor-following behaviour. If your home is small, mostly hard floor, and lightly used, a mid-range lidar model usually does the job a flagship does, just with more manual bin-emptying — money spent on the top tier there buys convenience, not cleanliness.

Can one robot vacuum handle tile, wood and carpet in a single run?

Most Irish homes aren't one floor type — hallway and kitchen tile, sitting-room wood or laminate, bedroom and stairs-landing carpet — and a robot crossing all three in one run needs to adjust suction and brush behaviour automatically rather than being set once and left. Manufacturer spec sheets increasingly list carpet-boost or auto-detect suction as a named feature, worth checking for specifically rather than assumed. Deep-pile or older carpet also traps more than surface dust: in Ireland's damp climate, carpet holds moisture readily, and combined with pet hair or dust it becomes a slow mould risk if it's only vacuumed occasionally. A robot running a proper daily or alternate-day schedule keeps that buildup down far better than a weekly manual vacuum, which is one of the stronger arguments for automation on carpeted Irish homes specifically, rather than the hard-floor-heavy houses the marketing photos usually show.

Who honours a robot vacuum warranty in Ireland after Brexit?

Since Brexit, where a brand actually fulfils orders and spare parts from matters more than it used to. Some robot vacuum brands sell into Ireland from UK warehouses, which can mean customs friction and delay on replacement filters, brushes or dock bags; others run EU distribution that ships parts from within the bloc without the same paperwork. Before buying, it's worth checking whether the retailer or brand has an Irish or EU-based support line and returns address, not just a UK one — a two-year warranty is only as useful as the ease of actually using it. Irish electronics retailers stocking a brand directly, rather than a marketplace reseller, generally handle warranty claims faster than going back to an overseas manufacturer. This matters more the further up the price range you go, since premium units have more that can go wrong and pricier parts to replace.

What does a robot vacuum really cost over three years?

The sticker price is the smallest number in a robot vacuum's real cost. Manufacturer figures and part-store pricing put annual consumables — main brush, side brushes, filters, and for self-empty models a supply of dock bags — somewhere between roughly €80 and €150 a year depending on tier and how often the model needs replacements. Mop-equipped machines add pad wear and, on self-washing docks, cleaning solution on top of that. Electricity for a dock left on standby year-round is modest but not zero. Stretched across three years of ownership, a budget machine bought for €200 and a mid-range one bought for €400 can end up closer in total cost than the purchase price suggests, once the budget model's shorter part lifespan and higher failure-replacement rate are factored in. Treat the box price as a deposit on an ongoing running cost, not the full transaction.

Frequently asked questions

Do I need a robot vacuum on every floor of a two-storey house?

No — most Irish two-storey homes get the most value from one unit on the ground floor, where daily foot traffic, kitchen crumbs and hallway grit accumulate fastest. Upstairs bedrooms and landings typically need vacuuming far less often, so a second robot is rarely worth the outlay unless there's a pet or heavy carpet upstairs too. Carrying a single unit up and down works in theory but rarely survives contact with a busy week — most owners who try it end up leaving the robot on one floor permanently within a month. If budget allows only one unit, put it where the mess actually happens, not where the carpet looks nicest.

Does Wi-Fi actually matter, or is the app just a gimmick?

For basic scheduling, no — most robots will run a fixed daily time without ever opening the app again. Wi-Fi and the app matter for two things specifically: room-by-room mapping with no-go zones, and remote start when you're not home, both useful in Irish homes with irregular hours or a spare room used for storage that should be skipped. It also matters for firmware updates, which occasionally fix real navigation bugs. A patchy router signal in a solid-walled older Irish house can cause disconnects and missed schedules more often than the vacuum itself is at fault, so check Wi-Fi coverage in the room the dock sits in before assuming a fault with the machine.

How noisy are robot vacuums, and does it matter for a small house?

Manufacturer figures typically list a range from around 55 dB on quiet or carpet-avoidance modes up to 65-70 dB on max suction — comparable to a conversation up to a running dishwasher. In a small terraced or semi-detached house, that's audible through most of the ground floor when it's running, which matters if you plan overnight or early-morning cleans near a bedroom close to the stairs. Running during the working day rather than at 6am avoids most complaints. Self-empty docks add a separate, louder burst of noise for 10-15 seconds during emptying, which some owners find more startling than the vacuum itself.

What happens to a robot vacuum if the power goes out mid-clean?

It stops wherever it is and resumes the same cleaning cycle once power returns and it's had a chance to redock and recharge, assuming it has enough map memory to remember where it left off — true of essentially all lidar and vision-mapping models sold today. Older bump-navigation budget models without persistent maps may just restart the whole clean from the dock. Irish power cuts are infrequent but not unheard of in rural or coastal areas during winter storms; it's a minor consideration, not a deciding one, but worth knowing it won't damage the unit or lose your saved map.

Should I wait for next year's model instead of buying now?

Robot vacuum navigation and mapping matured several years ago; the year-on-year changes now are mostly in obstacle-avoidance camera accuracy, dock automation and app polish rather than fundamental cleaning ability. A two- or three-year-old mid-range model with lidar mapping cleans a normal Irish home about as well as this year's equivalent. Waiting makes more sense if you specifically want a feature not yet available at your budget — cross-floor mapping memory, say — than as a general strategy, since prices on current-generation stock typically drop once a successor launches anyway.
